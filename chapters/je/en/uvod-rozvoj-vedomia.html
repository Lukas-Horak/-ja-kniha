The capacity for self-awareness was a pivotal evolutionary step that enabled individuals of various advanced animal species to become conscious of their own existence. At the same time, however, it created the aforementioned illusion of separateness and laid the foundation for the ego — the concept of "I" as an independent, separate entity that identifies with its own thoughts, emotions, and experiences, thereby becoming the centre of its own reality.

This illusion of separateness leads to feelings of loneliness, fear, and a constant need to affirm one's own worth. The individual perceives itself as the subject and everything else as objects with which it must communicate, coexist, compete, control, or resist. This sense of separation is not merely a source of negative emotional states and conflicts — it is also the foundation for the emergence of many complex psychological mechanisms and self-destructive behavioural patterns that are entirely commonplace in the general population.

With the development of consciousness, however, we begin to notice and recognise these mechanisms and behavioural patterns; we learn to observe them and discern their influence on our behaviour, decision-making, and perception. It is precisely the process of consciousness development that leads to the gradual overcoming of many limiting mechanisms and illusions, thereby opening up space for higher states of consciousness that allow us to better understand the true nature of reality.

The state of consciousness can be understood as a lasting or predominant mode of existence that determines how we perceive ourselves, the external world, and everything happening around us.

Unlike IQ, which is given for life and remains static, our state of consciousness can evolve throughout our lifetime.

Whether the state of consciousness is low or high profoundly affects the quality of our lives, our thinking, our behaviour, and our prevailing emotional state. It fundamentally influences what we experience in life, what problems we face, which people we "happen" to meet, and what outcomes we achieve. The lower the state of consciousness, the more frequently older evolutionary emotional states predominate — fear, craving, anger, guilt, shame, or even despair. In these states, a person cannot see reality clearly, because their perception is distorted by the ego's defence mechanisms.

When the state of consciousness is high, peace, harmony, trust, joy, gratitude, love, contentment, and similar qualities prevail. In the highest states of consciousness, life is regarded as perfect, and the spectrum of emotions experienced transcends ordinary human experience. These states of consciousness are, by their very nonlinear nature, indescribable in a positive sense of the word.

Conversely, a high IQ is no guarantee that a person will be happy, will not fall into depression, or will not experience emptiness, uncertainty, fear, anger, self-pity, shame, or guilt. Many highly intelligent people have succumbed to self-destructive behavioural patterns and addictions, or have found themselves at an existential nadir. History and the present alike offer us countless examples of geniuses and exceptionally gifted individuals who struggled with their own psyches, could not come to terms with traumas, failed to cope with life's emotional challenges, or even took their own lives. The fact is that intelligence alone does not lead to inner harmony, peace, serenity, contentment, or happiness. What is needed is a high degree of self-awareness, emotional maturity, and conscious work with one's inner states — thoughts and emotions. Without these qualities, high intelligence can even become a burden — our mind tends to analytically dissect reality into its smallest details, ponder complex problems, and ceaselessly conjure negative scenarios, which can lead to perpetual inner tension, chronic anxiety, or existential crises. A high intellect, though often regarded as one of the most valuable tools of human development, does not always work in our favour. Quite often, our intellect serves as a support for and rationalisation of primitive mechanisms.

Intelligence without a high degree of self-knowledge, emotional maturity, and developed consciousness can turn a person into a prisoner of their own mind — one who endlessly replays memories of the past, worries about the future, or ceaselessly dissects philosophical reflections and rational models about the meaning of life. The more complex our thinking, the more easily we can become entangled in uncontrolled spirals of thought. If the ability to consciously regulate inner experience is absent, even the sharpest individual can become a victim of their own internal processes.

It is therefore not at all unusual for people of average intellect to live harmonious, fulfilled, and contented lives, while many geniuses wrestle with themselves. Some narcissistically and self-adoringly say, "It's hard for me with my exceptional intellect"; others search for a way out.

Many intellectually brilliant individuals even look down on those who do not burden themselves daily with uncontrollable global problems, economic models, history, or philosophical theories. They regard with contempt those who live more simply, more intuitively, and in direct connection with life — as if their peace were a sign of ignorance or naivety.

True wisdom does not come from intellect, from quick thinking, from computing formulas, reciting facts, memorising data, or the ability to solve complex problems — it comes from deep self-knowledge.

Whoever truly comes to know themselves will know not only the universe, but the very Source of their existence.

It is highly unlikely that an individual's state of consciousness would fundamentally evolve during their lifetime without active effort. Consciousness does develop naturally, but primarily in its collective form — as a whole — and over the span of millions of years. In the context of an individual, it is not an automatic process that unfolds simply through the passage of time.

People often assume that the older a person is, the wiser or more conscious they become. In reality, many adults go through life trapped in the same unconscious behavioural patterns they operated in on the childhood playground or in early youth. For example, they repeatedly get into conflicts, react emotionally to the same situations, or continue to be governed by fear, anger, or craving. Over and over, they fight for their little bucket and shovel. Their perception of the world is tightly bound to survival, competition, possession, rivalry, and defending what is "mine" — my family, my group, my opinions, my territory, my position.

An individual's level of consciousness can even decline rapidly. In most cases, however, the level of consciousness in an ordinary person does not increase at all over the course of a lifetime. Without a conscious effort toward introspection, self-reflection, and growth, consciousness stagnates, and we can observe in people the same reactions to the same life situations, repeated endlessly — whether it is daily irritation at traffic, emotional outbursts about politics, incessant recollection of past grievances, or a lifelong game of victim and villain, and so on.

Equally true, however, is that if an individual sets out on the path of raising their level of consciousness and resolutely follows the principles for its development, they can achieve indescribable positive changes — or even experience the highest state of consciousness. Although this path is not easy, it is accessible to anyone who chooses to take it.

The development of consciousness is nonlinear in nature and therefore does not proceed in the way our mind expects. In other words, the output is not directly proportional to the input, as we anticipate in ordinary linear models. Over time, the results tend to be astonishing, influencing every area of our lives.

When we reflect more deeply, we may notice that never before in human history has a person had access to such a volume of information, stimuli, possibilities, and changes as today. In the past, life was slow, rhythmic, and predictable. An ordinary person experienced only a limited number of events, changes, or emotional encounters throughout an entire lifetime.

Today, however, we can experience more stimuli in a single day than our ancestors encountered in an entire year — or even an entire lifetime. We face constant challenges, crises, and conflicts, and our lives are profoundly shaped by ever-evolving technologies. We meet dozens of people daily, navigate complex life situations, absorb hundreds of pieces of information, and pass through an entire spectrum of emotions. Even something as innocent as a visit to the cinema allows us, within two hours, to "live through" a complex life story — love, betrayal, tension, fear, killing, grief, joy, loss, and triumph.

Our minds are exposed today to an extreme volume of stimuli. And it is precisely here that an enormous opportunity lies. The development of consciousness, which once could have taken decades of slow maturation in the silence of monasteries or desert solitudes, can today be radically accelerated. If an individual chooses to consciously process their experiences, to understand their essence and place them in the proper context, their inner development can advance at a tremendous pace. If they pause and ask themselves questions such as: "What is this situation teaching me?", "Why did I react the way I did?", "What is this triggering in me?", "What behavioural pattern is repeating here?" — an intensive development of consciousness takes place. Conversely, if a person experiences these stimuli only unconsciously, they stagnate and do not evolve.

The present era brings chaos, but at the same time an unprecedented potential for the transformation of consciousness.
