Anger is the result of unmet expectations, unfulfilled desires, and unaccepted aspects of our lives.

Anger, like resistance, has its roots in the early stages of evolutionary development of living organisms, when it served as a critical defense reaction.

In the simplest organisms, it was not emotion in the true sense, as we understand it in higher organisms or in humans. These were rather simple biological reactions to external stimuli. In the environment of primitive organisms, anger was essential for ensuring personal safety or protecting territory and offspring from predators. This transient emotional state allowed for quick and decisive reaction to threats, which could mean the difference between life and death.

In more complex organisms, the emotion of anger took on a more sophisticated form that includes not only aggressive defense but also serves as a means of establishing boundaries, maintaining control, or cooperation within a group.

For the modern human, however, anger often becomes problematic because the originally useful emotion can lead to destructive behavior. Although anger may continue to serve as a signal that our boundaries have been crossed or we have faced injustice, it often prevents personal or professional development because primitive reactions worsen interpersonal relationships and make it impossible to objectively resolve conflicts.

Overcoming the emotional state of anger and its transformation into more mature forms of expressing disagreement or managing conflicts is therefore key to achieving higher states of consciousness, where peace, tranquility, and constructive interaction with the environment dominate.

From a psychological perspective, anger is often activated when something or someone evokes a feeling in us that our interests, values, boundaries, or physical well-being are threatened. For our "Primitive Self," anger is the response to danger. It is one of the most basic emotional reactions that has its roots in primitive survival mechanisms, where the typical strategy is "fight or flight." In everyday life, anger manifests in various ways, for example in statements like: I can't believe you said that!, That's unfair to me! or You have no right to tell me what to do! These reactions are not just spontaneous emotional responses but often reflect inner vulnerability and fear of rejection or failure.

Anger can be beneficial in rare cases—if it motivates us to solve problems or defend our rights. However, we are talking about extreme situations where it is necessary to respond quickly to a threat, not ordinary daily interactions. For example, in defending against physical attack or protecting children and other vulnerable people, anger can be necessary. Another extreme example is deep injustice or ethical wrongdoing, when anger can lead to social change or brave acts that can help address social problems (for example, fighting for human rights).

In modern society, however, where various civilized ways exist to resolve conflicts, anger is not acceptable. This primitive expression is deeply rooted in survival instincts that made sense when people faced direct physical threats daily. If it is too frequent or intense, it is rather a sign of a low level of consciousness. Mature consciousness can process, regulate, and transform anger so that it supports positive change without unnecessary self-harm or harm to others.

In the context of consciousness development, it is important to understand that overcoming this emotional state goes far beyond managing impulsive outbursts of anger. It is about deeper overcoming of an inner state that tends to manifest as hidden frustration, constant complaining about something and someone, regular grumpiness, or outbursts of anger. This process requires intense self-reflection and inner work to identify the roots of these emotional states. They are often hidden in our unmet expectations, unfulfilled desires, and unaccepted aspects of our lives. Overcoming this primitive and reactive way of existence is therefore not just about the ability to not explode with anger every moment but mainly about identifying the source of our anger and frustration, and thus deepening our perception.

This process helps not only inner harmony and peace but also significant progress on the path to higher levels of consciousness, where negative emotional reactions are transcended and replaced with deeper understanding. Most people, however, do not have the ability to distinguish between projections of their mind and objective reality. The mind commonly confuses our personal interpretations of the world with objective external facts. In psychology, we encounter this phenomenon relatively often. What is actually only a collection of our thoughts, beliefs, opinions, expectations, and ideas (our internal view of the world) can be incorrectly identified by our mind as a precise and objective image of reality. However, someone who cannot recognize the difference between their subjective view of the world and the reality of the external world lives in constant suffering.

Much of this suffering arises because people project a certain idea of how things should function, and if they don't function that way, anger overtakes them. One could say that anger is their dominant state of consciousness. Everything upsets them, they constantly complain, throw their hands up, and in their constant frustration they gossip about others. They want something, and when they don't get it, they become angry. They desire something, and when it is not fulfilled, they become angry again. Their narcissistic ego can very easily confuse their personal desires with life needs.

Our "Primitive Self" constantly wants something, and when it doesn't achieve it, it tries through the energy of anger to bring about change. It simply wants to fulfill its desires and expectations, ideally as quickly as possible. This part of ourselves thinks we are the center of the universe. Discovering that we are not can easily cause prolonged frustration and inner anger. Thus anger becomes the predominant emotional state of an individual. We think that everyone should constantly fulfill our personal desires and subjective ideas about the world. However, this is not possible. When we realize this, prolonged frustration and anger toward the entire world grows in us because we didn't get what we wanted. Since the world around us does not correspond to our idealized notions, we often feel disappointed, misunderstood, frustrated, and dissatisfied. This anger is not just superficial or temporary; it is a deeply rooted emotional state that repeatedly causes frustration and affects our interactions with our surroundings.

At first, it is difficult to accept that there is no such thing as justified anger. Our "Primitive Self" wrongly thinks that for everyone we are the highest priority, everyone should agree with us, and everything should work in our favor. People dominated by this primitive view of the world expect their surroundings to fulfill all their desires, constantly make them happy, and adapt to their wishes. If this doesn't happen, it manifests in them as constant dissatisfaction, complaining, feeling offended, grumpiness, or outbursts of anger. Just like small children.

Anger is fundamentally a mechanism that was supposed to provide us with more energy and keep us simply alive. In the context of the survival of our ancestors, anger and an egocentric approach to life had great significance. If we wanted to survive, we had to think primarily of ourselves and our needs. However, our mind can very easily confuse desires, ideas, and expectations with real needs.

Whenever we shout, get upset, or attack someone, we behave like a primitive animal trying to look bigger or more frightening. This deceptive move can often be seen in nature in various animal species. Snakes, octopuses, birds, bears, crabs, or cats do this, for example. Just as with other animals, in the case of humans, it is merely an attempt to deter the enemy or camouflage our shortcomings. Although it is understandable that such behavior is useful in the wild, in human society these reactions are often inappropriate, disrupt interpersonal relationships, and cause us unhappiness. Aware people consider anger a weakness. They don't feel good if they notice this evolutionarily oldest emotion in themselves even for a moment. As soon as they catch themselves being angry, they try to quickly calm down and act more rationally. They don't let anger grow in them. They don't stay stuck in anger. They realize that anger is not a sign of strength but a manifestation of the weakness of immature individuals.

Because of unmet expectations and distorted ideas about how things should function, inner anger tends to grow in people over years to such an extent that it causes self-destructive behavior. Inner conflict deepens in them, a sense of failure or disappointment in themselves, which can easily develop into unconscious self-hate and unconscious sabotage of their own happiness, such as destroying loving relationships, excessive use of alcohol and intoxicating substances, overeating, excessive risk-taking, self-harm, but also inappropriate communication with superiors, avoidance of opportunities, workaholism leading to burnout, and the like.

The consequences that result from some of these behavioral patterns tend to be catastrophic. They can lead to the destruction of relationships, health problems, or work complications. Sometimes professional help is needed, which can be the first step in identifying the causes of this self-hatred and subsequent healing. Our "Primitive Self," however, considers the admission of inner problems as a weakening of its position, as a sign of weakness, as a "loss," and so in some cases is willing to rather end long-standing friendships or partnerships, especially when it can no longer hide its inner shortcomings from these people. It is much simpler to avoid confronting one's inner problems than to acknowledge them and actively address them. To be able to address them, we need to have a high degree of self-reflection, humility, and willingness to acknowledge our emotional problems, which can be very deep.

Some people fear that healing their inner traumas will lead to a change in their personality. However, healing does not lead to a change in personality. A person becomes who they truly are. In psychology, this process is called individuation. Reassessing destructive patterns of one's thinking and behavior, having the courage to take responsibility for them, showing a healthy measure of regret and apologizing—all of this requires a higher level of consciousness than most of the population possesses.

It is completely normal that inner anger manifests outwardly in some individuals. Sometimes it comes in mild forms of anger in more acceptable forms, such as complaining or grumpiness. Other times there are less acceptable outbursts of anger, verbal attacks, insults, or "being offended to death." Psychologists claim that egocentric and narcissistic personality types are not even capable of forgiving. Just like small children, these individuals tend to understand the world exclusively from their own perspective. If someone hurts their ego, they may have difficulty forgiving because they focus primarily on how they feel themselves and not on how others feel. They cannot cope with negative experiences in a healthy and constructive way and tend toward aggressive and impulsive behavior. This is caused by the prevailing mechanisms of the "Primitive Self," which prevent them from understanding, compassion, empathy, and unconditional love. Sometimes egocentric and narcissistic behavior is so strong that people reject loving life partners, long-time friends, or even their own children because of their unmet ideas and expectations. Instead of approaching their loved ones with understanding, empathy, and love, they prefer non-forgiveness and bitterness. They cannot forgive even when someone apologizes repeatedly, because their "Primitive Self" delights in drama, conflict, and negativity.

For people with lower consciousness, manifestations of anger, revenge, non-forgiveness, and aggression are signs of strength. Forgiveness, understanding, and humility seem to them like weakness. Yet the opposite is true. Anger and other strong emotional reactions such as fear and anxiety are evolutionary remnants that helped us survive in primitive conditions of the animal world. In the past, they had significance; today they prevent us from developing. It is only up to us to learn how to handle them healthily in the modern world.

Our "Primitive Self," however, literally revels in "righteous" indignation, a sense of injustice, or thoughts of revenge. It causes us a kind of satisfaction and psychological pleasure that we don't want to give up. Some people can be upset about injustice from the past for ten, twenty, or thirty years, comfortably for a whole life. They repeatedly tell how someone hurt them. We agreed he would do it that way, and he did it differently!, Why didn't he tell me such important information? He betrayed me!, How could he say this and that then?! I will never forgive him!

Such dwelling on the past can provide us with temporary satisfaction because it allows us to feel morally superior to the person who "hurt" us. However, it is a type of psychological pleasure with a short-term effect that leads to long-term negative consequences—it creates chronic stress, constant tension, negative emotions, physical health problems, and can destroy interpersonal relationships.

Imagine that for the next thirty years you will be dealing with the fact that someone did not meet your expectations. They didn't fulfill your idea. They dared to do something differently than you wanted. It is voluntary imprisonment in one specific moment, which constantly causes us unpleasant feelings. This moment remains in our mind like an unhealed wound that constantly reminds itself. Instead of trying to understand, focusing on the present, and letting the past be the past, in our head we repeatedly play negative moments, repeat random snippets of conversation, invent all possible alternative scenarios, and thus constantly burden our minds with nonsense.

People dominated by the emotional state of anger remain stuck in the same place. Anger connects us with another person, place, or specific events, which constantly keeps us in a negative pattern of thinking and behavior. Until we resolve our inner anger, our lives will repeatedly experience situations that trigger our anger. We will repeatedly meet people with qualities that upset us. It will repeat until our view of the world fundamentally changes. Then, as if by miracle, these situations, these people, these places disappear from our lives. We find that it was always just about our inner attitude, not about what's happening outside. Although today it might be unimaginable to someone, this change in perspective and way of thinking brings enormous liberation.

People with dominant "Primitive Self" repeatedly talk about how someone betrayed them, deceived them, lied to them, disappointed them, or treated them inappropriately. That is also why they cannot move forward in their lives to a more successful, harmonious, and peaceful life. They are literally stuck. This stuckness in the past imprisons the person in negative patterns of thinking and behavior, which prevents personal development and achieving emotional maturity. If a person is unable to let go of negative memories, they cannot live in the present.

People dominated by the emotional state of anger repeatedly get upset over trivial things—from long lines in stores to unwanted ads during their favorite series to political decisions that they perceive as personal attacks. They are upset by traffic situations, noisy neighbors, or unpleasant colleagues at work. They complain about weather fluctuations or that their favorite cafe changed its menu. They often vent their anger on social networks, where they spend hours sharing their frustrations, regardless of their low significance in the broader context of life. They repeatedly talk about how they were disadvantaged by the system, political decisions, business partners, society, and how they were really betrayed by the whole world. It won't be long before we hear how someone prevented their success, how they were betrayed, disappointed, lied to, abused, exploited, robbed, overcharged, bribed, overvalued, neglected, ignored, or condemned.

If we notice even the slightest hint of these manifestations in ourselves, it is time to consider an alternative approach to life. At the next unnecessary conflict, we should ask ourselves:

Do I really need to look for enemies? Do I have to constantly argue with someone? Is it necessary to constantly be offended like a small child? Do I need to ruin my day for my subjective feelings? Don't I already have enough negativity in my life without it? Is there really no better way to think? Wouldn't it be simpler in every moment to forgive, understand, and feel compassion?
