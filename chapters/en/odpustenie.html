Forgiveness is a gift we give ourselves. It is an act of enormous strength that weak people are incapable of.

The inability to forgive is closely linked to the emotion of anger. It is a complex phenomenon rooted in the old structures of our brain, in emotional states, social patterns, and also in the individual psychological state. From the perspective of evolutionary psychology, the inability to forgive is equally associated with mechanisms that were advantageous in the past for survival and reproduction.

We can observe these mechanisms today in many socially complex animals, primarily those that display a certain form of social hierarchy. For example, higher primates, such as chimpanzees, cannot easily forgive aggression or betrayal within their group because such behavior could threaten their status or safety. This mechanism is common in wolves, elephants, and some birds, but has its roots in ancient times. Animals that left attacks unanswered were perceived as weak. If they easily forgave the aggressive behavior of other animals, couldn't recognize the aggressor and couldn't determine boundaries, they could become repeated victims of aggression. Non-forgiveness thus served as a defense mechanism that prevented repeated attacks and exploitation.

It is natural that in harsh nature, animals must prevent such behavior to minimize the possibility of injury and thus increase their chance of survival. In ancient communities, where competition and social status were key to access to resources and mating partners, the act of non-forgiveness represented an act of strength. At the same time, it was a way to maintain one's position in the social hierarchy. Conversely, forgiveness appeared as a weakness. To maintain one's position, it was more advantageous to not forgive and even to take treacherous revenge so that no one would dare do anything similar.

In the modern world, the mechanism of non-forgiveness is highly impractical and counterproductive. From the perspective of an individual's consciousness development, we can consider it a major obstacle. It significantly burdens the psyche, inhibits the ability to establish new or maintain existing positive relationships, and fundamentally limits the ability of the individual to experience inner peace, tranquility, happiness, and well-being. It is also usually the main cause of stagnation in the area of consciousness development. People with a more advanced level of consciousness consider manifestations of anger and inability to forgive as a weakness and, conversely, value and respect precisely the ability to forgive. They even try to inspire others to rid themselves of their emotional suffering in the same way.

The inability to forgive is part of complex dynamics of individual and group behavior. Non-forgiveness can serve others in the group as a signal that certain behavior is not tolerated, which leads to higher stability within the group. Aggression, anger, revenge, and inability to forgive—all of these are qualities of the "Primitive Self" that have a destructive impact on our physical, mental, and spiritual health.

We often encounter the cycle of harm, anger, and revenge in the film industry. Revenge killing is, at least on the movie screen, even tolerated and welcomed.

Non-forgiveness keeps us in emotional pain and leads to continuous experiencing of negative feelings. Constantly remembering an attack, insult, betrayal, or injustice is enormously exhausting. A person who remains in a cycle of shame, guilt, self-pity, sadness, frustration, and anger consciously or unconsciously chooses negativity and suffering.

It is precisely forgiveness that allows us to free ourselves from pain and begin the healing process. Some will claim: That cannot be forgiven. Which in translation simply means: I don't want to forgive. If it truly couldn't be forgiven, countless people before us who experienced the same or much worse and still forgave wouldn't have accomplished it.

Refusing to forgive or constantly pointing to some injustice from the past is a choice. It is not at all uncommon for people to unconsciously "revel" in their suffering and refuse to let it go at any cost. They are willing to talk about injustice from the past for years, decades, or even a whole life. In such cases, it is often about the well-known "victim game" or "victim mentality," which is the foundation of a self-created identity as a "victim." It is a defense mechanism or a way of behaving when an individual perceives themselves as a victim of circumstances and tends to shift responsibility for their problems to other people. These "victims" repeatedly tell how they were hurt, which in certain social circles brings them attention, compassion, and support from others. Sometimes their expression is full of self-pity; other times they falsely seek to provoke even admiration: Look at me. I was betrayed, but I'm here.

The victim game brings rewards to the individual not only on a psychological level but also in a real sense. Pretending to be a victim and letting others help is energetically much simpler than taking responsibility for one's own life. Victim mentality represents a way of protecting oneself from feelings of shame, guilt, or failure, which are very unpleasant emotional states that a person wants to avoid at all costs. If they take the position of a victim, they don't have to face responsibility for their failure, don't have to feel ashamed, and don't have to take any corrective action. Of course, it's simpler to deceive oneself: They are to blame! Certainly not me. Forgiving the "guilty" could be seen as losing the identity of a "victim," as well as all the benefits associated with it. The person would suddenly have to take responsibility for their life, which requires great courage, self-reflection, and humility. Most people cannot take this step. They don't have enough strength for it.

In some cases, victim mentality can be a way of coping with deep trauma, but this is usually a manifestation of a deeper psychological problem.

In general, behavior associated with playing the victim and the perpetrator occurs in modern society relatively often, but intensity can vary. At the level of ordinary consciousness, there must always be "this" that causes "that," which is natural causal perception and the search for cause-and-effect relationships. There is always some perpetrator, culprit, or oppressor.

The life stories of most people are based on drama: They are to blame for what happened to me. Look at me, how I was wronged. There is always a culprit to be found who causes harm to the innocent.

Developed consciousness transcends this dualistic division of people, situations, and events into opposing categories of "good" and "bad," and such an individual also transcends the search for causes in the external world. As a result, space opens up for deeper understanding and perception of reality, where it is no longer necessary to condemn anyone, play the victim, or superficially evaluate life events.

The inability to forgive and take responsibility for one's failures, shortcomings, mistakes, or life in general is also linked to complex psychological interactions.

In modern society, we can commonly observe complex interpersonal interactions that fulfill not only the victim but also their "rescuers." Such individuals unconsciously enjoy psychological pleasure in the form of self-aggrandizement, admiration, or a sense of importance. Poor thing, how you were hurt. Come, we will help you. It is a sophisticated mechanism of ego that we can observe fairly often in society—usually called rescuer syndrome or savior syndrome. This interaction has its roots in primitive societies, where survival was usually a matter of cooperation with others. From the perspective of evolutionary psychology, the victim game can be perceived as a strategic tactic that stimulates empathy and helps gain support from the environment. On the other hand, individuals with rescuer syndrome thus gained social and reproductive advantages. By showing their ability and willingness to provide help, they raised their social status. In other cases, it is simply a psychological gain that gives the rescuer pleasant feelings and an improved self-image.

The victim game and rescuer syndrome are complex psychological mechanisms that have roots in our evolutionary history and, at first glance, can bring certain advantages. As mentioned, the victim game provides individuals with attention, empathy, or even material support from their surroundings, which can seem calming for a short time. Similarly, rescuer syndrome increases the social status of those engaged in helping and thus provides them with a certain form of social or even reproductive advantages. However, we must understand that both mechanisms have their dark sides.

The victim game leads to rejection of personal responsibility, emotional dependency, and sometimes even masks real, lasting, and deep problems. Rescuer syndrome, on the other hand, tends to lead to emotional exhaustion, unrealistic expectations, and the weakening of those we are trying to help. In both cases, it is an unsustainable model of behavior that keeps people in negative emotions, provides them with superficial and false psychological gains, and disrupts healthy interpersonal relationships. Recognizing these mechanisms and their negative consequences is key so we can grow and develop healthier interactions with our environment.

Forgiving someone who has hurt us, wronged us, or prevented us requires overcoming evolutionarily old and negative emotional states, such as anger, sadness, or guilt. These low emotions provide us with a certain form of satisfaction, but in reality, they prevent us from reaching higher states of consciousness. The false psychological gain we derive from these negative emotions is a complex interplay of psychological and neurological factors.

On the psychological level, the emotion of guilt (blaming someone for something) can provide us with a temporary feeling of justice and control over the situation. He, she, it is guilty. I am the victim.

Self-pity, on the other hand, gives us a sense of recognition or comfort because we can continue to play the victim and gain sympathy from others. Ultimately, however, we always get stuck in a helpless state. From a neurological perspective, self-pity can even trigger the release of dopamine, which leads to temporary feelings of satisfaction, even though the overall emotional state remains negative. It is an incredibly sophisticated mechanism that is difficult for a person to detect in themselves. Our "Primitive Self," in combination with reason, can come up with very good arguments why forgiveness is not possible, and the cycle of guilt, anger, and self-pity can become even addictive, which prevents us from moving to higher states of consciousness.

Let's imagine that we have a beautiful relationship and a loving partner, but we constantly return to moments that caused us unpleasant feelings. We were wronged. Something happened differently than we wanted. Instead of understanding, forgiveness, and love, we decide to stay trapped in the past. It makes us feel good. It gives us a false sense of justice, moral superiority, and control over our partner. The inability to forgive means unwillingness to forgive. It is a choice. If it didn't make us feel "good," we wouldn't spoil the present moment for the sake of the past and wouldn't destroy our relationships. We wouldn't sabotage our own happiness. However, millions of people, for the evolutionary, psychological, and biological reasons already mentioned, prefer to remain stuck in negative cycles of behavior that often lead to damage or complete breakdown of interpersonal relationships.

The remedy for overcoming these destructive behavioral patterns is understanding, but also deep self-reflection and radical humility. When we honestly acknowledge that we too can be trapped in the same behavioral pattern, we can freely choose to leave it. By merely observing our own thoughts and emotions, we change their nature and impact on our lives. We create space around them that puts us in the position of an observer and allows us to deeper understand ourselves. If we notice that we prefer to regularly spoil moments of love and moments of harmony because we simply cannot forgive, we are probably only enjoying the psychological gain that non-forgiveness brings us. The act of observation becomes a transformative process that can cause a leap in consciousness. Suddenly we see everything in different contexts and negative thoughts no longer have the same impact as before. As soon as we consciously decide to end this behavioral pattern, our life changes immediately. Liberation from emotional suffering arrives and we can live not only in greater harmony with the surrounding world but also achieve greater inner peace.

A low level of consciousness prevents people from understanding that the mind is full of errors, false beliefs, destructive programs, and pathological behavioral patterns.

A higher level of consciousness allows us to understand that positive and negative moments are part of human existence and often it is the negative ones that are the foundation for becoming better, more compassionate, more empathetic, happier, and more successful people. They do not serve the purpose of us returning in emotional development to primitive states of resistance, anger, blind accusation, or hatred. We simply need to forgive. Once and for all. Everyone, including ourselves. Forgiveness is not only a path to emotional healing but also to physical and spiritual healing. Chronic stress caused by anger, vengeful thoughts, and inability to forgive leads to numerous health problems such as high blood pressure, heart disease, and sleep problems. Forgiveness, on the other hand, helps not only relieve stress but has an overall beneficial effect on our health.

The process of forgiveness may seem difficult and painful, but the benefits it brings are invaluable. The higher consciousness we develop, the more we understand connections and context, which leads to a deeper understanding of these "unforgivable" events and situations.

At some point, we won't even be able to figure out what we need to forgive. We will realize that every human being does the best they can in any given moment. If they could have behaved better, they would have. The same applies to us. If we had the knowledge at a specific time that we have available now, we would have behaved differently. However, we didn't have this information, so we couldn't behave differently. What sense is there in disagreeing with how the universe works? Everything happens as it should. The universe is as it should be.

Dwelling in guilt, self-pity, or anger won't help anyone. We cannot change the past. The only thing we can change is how we look at the world today.

In this second, we can decide to forgive everyone who has hurt us in life. Absolutely everyone without exception. We truly forgive and wish them only the best in life. It is an immensely powerful inner process that can change our life in an unimaginable way. In this second, we can get rid of the pain, suffering, and negative emotions that would otherwise destroy us for the rest of our lives.

Ultimately, forgiveness is a gift we give ourselves. It is not a sign of weakness. It is an act of enormous strength that weak people are incapable of. It is a conscious choice that allows us to move forward and live a happier, more peaceful, more joyful, and more meaningful life.

Guilt and Its Purpose

The primary purpose of guilt is to learn from mistakes and take corrective action. Dwelling in guilt is merely self-punishment.

Our "enemy" need not be somewhere "out there." The one we cannot forgive can be ourselves.

If we experience deep inner guilt and cannot forgive ourselves, our entire life may be accompanied by low self-esteem and low self-love, leading to destructive behavior and constant sabotage of our own success and happiness.

We all know how unpleasant guilt can be. Sometimes it feels like sudden prickling in the chest, anxiety, or localized pain. Sometimes it works like an invisible poison devouring us from within. Other times it is a quiet voice constantly reminding us of our failures and unforgivable mistakes. We feel guilt when our actions or deliberate inaction are not in harmony with our subjective ethical code or social norms.

In general, the lower our consciousness level and the more active our "Primitive Self," the more frequently we experience negative emotions. A person at the level of ordinary consciousness is constantly struck by waves of various emotions, yet guilt tends to be so unpleasant that it demands immediate attention.

Like other emotions, guilt is a deep inner experience that cannot be precisely described, measured, or quantified, yet we all agree it is real and very unpleasant.

Whenever we realize we have violated moral, social, or personal standards, a complex mechanism behind guilt can be triggered. The standards from which we rebound are imprints of our inherited beliefs, instilled values, and personal experiences that shape our worldview and influence our perception. When we find ourselves on the other side of these invisible boundaries, guilt sounds as an urgent alarm forcing us to reevaluate our actions.

This inner mechanism had enormous significance in the evolution of social species, including humans. Guilt played a key role in forming and maintaining primitive social structures, and continues to strengthen social cohesion and motivate individuals toward responsible behavior. In this context, guilt was an invaluable tool in the evolution of social relationships and even played a significant role in developing higher emotional states such as compassion and empathy. Higher emotions have an immense positive effect on society, helping any group prosper and develop in mutual harmony.

This evolutionarily old emotional state, which played a key role in the survival and development of social species, must today be replaced by higher, more developed, and more mature emotional states that better suit the modern world.

Let's examine how this emotion developed throughout evolution.

At the level of the simplest living organisms, emotions like guilt are not present. As mentioned, the simplest life responds primarily to chemical and physical stimuli from the environment but is incapable of experiencing complicated inner states.

More complex living organisms exhibit basic social behavior, such as cooperation within groups or territorial behavior, but equally cannot experience complex emotional states.

True social behavior appears in more complex mammals. Such behavior includes hierarchy, cooperation among group members, and primitive forms of empathy or self-reflection, which are necessary prerequisites for consciousness development. Without guilt, an individual could not perceive and evaluate their own actions in relation to other group members, and thus this now less practical emotion played an important role in evolutionary development.

In ancient human societies, from the Stone Age through tribal societies to ancient civilizations, guilt functioned as a significant protective mechanism that increased chances of maintaining cohesion and order within a group. In these early eras, when legal systems and formal laws did not yet exist or were not fully developed, guilt helped regulate inappropriate behavior and strengthen group cooperation. It enabled individuals to feel responsibility for unacceptable actions (such as violence toward group members, disrespect for ritual rules, failure to fulfill hunting duties, or disruption of sexual relationships). Guilt acted as an inner regulator of behavior, inclining individuals to adhere to socially accepted norms and established rules. Those who acted against these rules experienced guilt, which motivated them not only to repair their behavior but also to avoid future transgressions. This intense and unpleasant inner feeling helped ensure that group members more often behaved in accordance with its expectations. Thus, guilt in ancient times increased not only individual survival chances but also those of the entire group.

Guilt acts as a signal that "what I am doing" or "what I have done" is not tolerated and will not be accepted. In various periods and cultures: If I continue to behave this way, I risk my standing in the hierarchy, exclusion from the group, punishment, or death. The emotional state of guilt thus had high informational value for individuals. If excluded from the group, their survival chances decreased significantly. Guilt contributed to individuals repairing their mistakes and avoiding conflict or exclusion, because this was very dangerous. An individual cut off from the community lost access to resources and protection the collective provided.

For the group, it was advantageous to exclude individuals with "irreparable" behavior, as they posed a threat. However, if such behavior improved, the group maintained cohesion and did not lose a member (hunter, gatherer, childcare provider, warrior, fisherman, shaman, etc.), which had a positive effect on community survival and prosperity. Guilt increased the group's ability to adapt to the environment and face external challenges as a unified collective.

Our "Primitive Self" remembers that more group members meant greater defensive force against tribal attacks and more efficient hunts, which directly affected survival chances. Being part of a group was necessary for survival. This is one reason why older brain structures such as the limbic system, governing basic emotional reactions, combined with psychological mechanisms, may subconsciously incline us to dwell in guilt. These evolutionarily older structures ensure survival through "automatic" emotional reactions. In the modern world, however, these mechanisms can lead to unjustified dwelling in negative emotional states that no longer have the same adaptive significance.

In today's society, there is no direct dependence on safety and resources provided by groups. Progress in technology, science, and organized social structures, modern healthcare and social systems, laws, rules, and resource access enable individuals to survive relatively independently from their social circle size. Many threats that once required constant group cooperation are no longer real. This does not mean social bonds or group support are unnecessary today, but their purpose has changed. Being part of a group is now more about emotional support and social comfort than physical survival.

Dwelling in guilt is rather a relic of our "Primitive Self," which inflates the importance of this negative emotional state.

In the religious context of Western culture, guilt is often placed on a pedestal. Guilt and sin historically served as tools to educate or deter people from undesired behavior. Churches and government institutions abused them in certain periods to manipulate public opinion and solidify their power.

When we understand life's evolution and become aware of how our "Primitive Self" developed, we will perceive guilt and sin rather as relics of the past. Burdening individuals with guilt and shame for actions not directly under their control or natural aspects of human existence is unhelpful. Some behaviors considered "sinful" or unnatural are deeply rooted in our biological and psychological mechanisms and in certain contexts may still be useful. Expecting we can completely rid ourselves of them is naive and unrealistic.

In modern humans, guilt has reached full complexity, particularly due to higher cognitive abilities, increased self-awareness, more complicated interpersonal relationships, and more complex social structures. The modern human lives in a comparatively more complicated world than predecessors. This complexity has significantly changed how we live, work, and communicate. Moreover, the modern human must adhere to numerous ethical and moral rules that did not exist in the past.

Social media plays a major role here, enabling people to glimpse into others' lives and creating new social expectations, often leading to guilt, self-criticism, anxiety, and depression. These platforms create an idealized image of lives in which individuals constantly smile, have no problems, are beautiful, successful, and full of adventure. Of course, this does not reflect actual reality.

The notion that someone pulls out their phone, places an artificial smile on their face for a photograph, and then returns to their actual mood is theatrical absurdity. What people watch on social networks is often a perfectly arranged illusion with little in common with reality and a mockery of true authenticity. Individuals consuming such content may subsequently perceive their own lives as less successful or less fulfilled, leading to inner dissatisfaction and guilt.

A constant influx of idealized images from others' lives unfortunately leads many people to low emotional states. They envy, despise, mock, feel jealousy, disgust, or long for the same.

Guilt arrives not only when we fail to meet high social expectations set by these illusions but also when we openly or silently gossip about and diminish those who create such content.

On a deeper level, we are well aware we have no right to judge or condemn anyone. Gossip, mocking criticism, and diminishing others stems from the most primitive parts of our psyche—a product of the "Primitive Self," which attempts to increase its own value by diminishing that of others. At a deeper, more aware, and more developed level of consciousness—our "Higher Self"—there exists a contradiction between this primitive behavior and more mature approaches to life. This inner contradiction leads to guilt, because our "Higher Self" does not support the critical and judgmental thoughts the "Primitive Self" produces. We feel guilty because deep down we know that gossip, criticism, or diminishment of others is not in harmony with higher values and highest principles of life. Whenever we behave this way, we are automatically "punished" by inner guilt. Essentially, we harm ourselves. Our primitive attitudes often lead to inner disharmony, disruption of the body's biological functions, and emotional unrest.

Few realize that guilt has enormous negative impact on our physical health. Long-term dwelling in this negative emotional state leads to elevated levels of stress hormones cortisol and adrenaline, increased blood pressure, disrupted acid-base balance, and poor sleep quality, which weakens our immune system and disrupts various biological processes. These statements are not just logically understandable but scientifically demonstrable.

Even brief thoughts of fear, guilt, or anger can weaken our ability to resist adverse environmental factors. When people become ill, they almost always seek the cause in the external world—a cold drink, inappropriate clothing, or a colleague who coughed nearby. Due to their ignorance, it never occurs to them that the cause may be hidden within—in the negative emotional states recently experienced. Few understand that self-critical thoughts, guilt, envy, or fits of rage from a few days ago could significantly affect body systems and cause decreased immunity. The "Primitive Self" always seeks causes outside itself. There must always be some culprit who caused what happened to us.

When we persist long-term in evolutionarily older emotional states of guilt, fear, self-criticism, or self-pity, we create favorable conditions for complicated health problems. Long-term negative thoughts truly devour us from within. These negative emotional states negatively affect our biological functions and contribute to various diseases, including cardiovascular and autoimmune diseases, increased inflammation, depressive states, or even cancer. People will say, I drank too much alcohol or I didn't eat healthily. Few consider the hours, weeks, or months a person spent in thoughts of self-pity, self-criticism, self-blame, or self-hatred.

Even with complicated health problems, people usually shift responsibility to external factors instead of considering their long-term negative inner states or behavior. I have heart problems because it runs in the family. It's genetic. I became diabetic, it's just bad luck. Or: My condition worsened because doctors didn't catch my disease in time.

Such thoughts reflect the human tendency to avoid taking responsibility for one's own health and to attribute illness causes to external factors—ancestors, healthcare, doctors, or chance.

An even crueler truth is that when someone persists in guilt long-term, they may subconsciously believe they deserve to become ill, creating a vicious circle very difficult to break.

Such thinking is not merely self-punishment but a way a person unconsciously accepts suffering as a deserved consequence of previous actions. The more inner guilt we feel, the more sinister a disease we are willing to accept. What we hold in our minds tends to manifest.

In the context of consciousness development, guilt is a double-edged sword, because this emotion had significance primarily under conditions in harsh nature and the animal world.

On one hand, guilt can motivate individuals to repair mistakes and behave more ethically, laying the foundation for self-reflection, compassion, and empathy.

On the other hand, if guilt is disproportionate or prolonged, it negatively affects the physical, emotional, and mental health of the individual, often triggers self-destructive behavioral patterns, leads to sabotage of one's own happiness and success, and in extreme cases even to passive suicide. This is behavior in which an individual does not directly attempt to end their life but subconsciously ignores or neglects basic health or safety measures, which can lead to death—overeating, smoking, regularly drinking alcohol, long-term drug use, deliberately stopping life-essential medications, or regularly crossing the street without looking. An attentive driver may witness such behavior almost daily, and it is not limited to elderly people, the homeless, or drunkards.

The primary purpose of guilt is to learn from mistakes and take corrective action, so we do not repeat them.

Dwelling in guilt is self-punishment, because a person believes they deserve to suffer for past wrongdoings. If they repeatedly blame themselves, they may develop a false sense that they are emotionally "paying" for their mistakes. This creates a temporary illusion of balance or justice, even though it leads to persistent negative emotional states bringing only guilt, anger, self-pity, self-hatred, and self-punishment. By dwelling in guilt, we help no one and certainly do not "pay" for anything.

Dwelling in guilt brings no positive results and does not serve as an effective remedy for mistakes. True remedy requires active steps toward change and improvement, not passive entrapment in negative states.

Inner guilt can become an unbearable burden that quietly limits our development, prevents constructive thinking, and significantly prevents happiness.

It is important to honestly reflect on whether in our case guilt is a way to keep ourselves in harmony with our moral compass, or whether it is truly merely voluntary self-punishment.

Even with brief self-study of how the human brain functions, we can discover that our failures stem from natural limitations of the human mind. They are nothing unique or exceptional or typical to one person but a universal characteristic of our species, stemming from a common pattern of thinking and behavior.

Instead of self-pity, self-hatred, and self-blame, we can move forward or even help others avoid similar mistakes.

The boundary at which guilt is triggered differs from person to person. Each individual has their boundaries set at different places, and therefore experiencing guilt is always strongly subjective. There is no objectively established universal boundary, because this boundary is influenced by personal values, cultural background, upbringing, and unique life experiences.

We created an image of ourselves, and after our failure, this image shattered. We cannot forgive ourselves. Sometimes we narcissistically indulge in the belief that our particular mistake is incredibly serious and unforgivable and that we are the ones who made it. How could I have done that? Why did I have to fail? The "Primitive Self" constantly wants to narcissistically draw attention to itself and its suffering. I made the mistake. No one else. Me. I am suffering.

Yet what is unforgivable for one person can be acceptable for another.

The better we understand primitive mechanisms of our minds and human nature, the less significance this emotion holds.

An individual with advanced consciousness knows dwelling in guilt is destructive and helps no one. This does not mean being indifferent to destructive behavior—quite the opposite. Such a person understands that it is natural for humans and that guilt's only purpose is to learn from mistakes and take corrective action. They also know that dwelling in guilt has pathological effects on life.

It is important not to sink into self-contempt and remain trapped in destructive emotional cycles, but to use mistakes as opportunities for improvement.

The first step is to learn from the mistake. We must understand more deeply what led to it. This process involves placing the event in a new context. We need to view the situation from an observer's perspective and try to understand it in broader contexts. With self-reflection, introspection, and humility, we can better understand what mistakes and poor decisions contributed to the unwanted outcome. It is important to focus on ourselves and not seek blame elsewhere. This step is primarily about learning and growth, not destructive self-judgment.

The second step is to forgive ourselves. This step is often hardest, because it requires overcoming our inner voice that continually criticizes and reminds us of our failures. Yet self-forgiveness is not only the most difficult but also the most important step. If it does not occur, we remain in negative thoughts and suffer emotionally. After sincere acknowledgment of our mistake, forgiveness is the step that allows us to heal emotionally and free ourselves from the chains of the past.

After identifying mistakes and forgiving ourselves, it is beneficial to take corrective action—apologize, communicate openly, attempt to mitigate negative consequences, take full responsibility for our actions, and actively work on improving ourselves.

The final step is continuing our development, which gives the guilt mechanism meaning. The purpose of this emotional state is fulfilled.

We must realize that we always do the best we can. If we could think differently, we would. If we could behave differently, we would. We always act according to the best possible judgment available to us at any moment. That is reality. Arguing over "what could have been if" is merely wasting time and energy. People waste unnecessary time considering what they could have done differently. Contemplating the past through "if only" leads only to endless cycles of guilt, shame, and regret.

Each action and result have their place and purpose. Each mistake and negative experience contributes to our growth. Each moment in our lives serves as a step on the path to greater self-awareness. Each experience, good or bad, moves us toward greater understanding of ourselves and the world around us. We all experience positive and negative situations, regardless of age, experience, or position. Negative experiences are a natural part of human existence. If we handle them correctly, they can positively influence our future decisions and help us become more mature, wiser, and more developed individuals.

My Inner Child

Our reactions to various life situations are often influenced by emotional wounds from childhood, and therefore can be immature and inappropriate.

Many adults have behavioral patterns resembling how small children behave. Constant stubbornness, jealousy, envy, inability to admit mistakes, inability to manage anger, low self-esteem, constant creation of artificial conflicts, disrespect for others' opinions, or lack of empathy tend to be caused by emotional immaturity. These patterns may be unconsciously used as a defensive mechanism protecting the vulnerable part of our personality—our "inner child".

Unresolved emotional needs or emotional traumas from childhood that influence adult behavior are typically addressed by therapists through the concept of the inner child. It is widely recognized in psychology and psychotherapy and helps us better understand emotional and behavioral problems rooted in unresolved childhood experiences. When this mechanism activates in adulthood, reactive behavior can occur that is not aligned with the rational or mature approach expected from adults. Although such behavior is a common part of human psychology and understandable in terms of psychological defense, it often conflicts with modern social life's requirements, which demands more controlled and thoughtful reactions.

In the context of consciousness development, work with these aspects of our psychology is important for achieving emotional maturity and higher levels of consciousness.

In moments when our inner world becomes a small theater with scenes reminiscent of childhood, we can tell ourselves: Aha, this is my inner child\\! In these moments, one simple and highly self-reflective question could help our development: Why am I behaving like a small child? Or: What causes this behavior? It is precisely self-reflection that helps us understand and process our hidden emotions and traumas and enable us the path toward mature, balanced, adult behavior.

On the psychological concept of the inner child, Swiss psychiatrist and founder of analytical psychology Carl Jung also spoke. According to him, it is important to bring our inner child out of the unconscious and include it in our consciousness so we can gain emotional balance and better cope with childhood wounds and traumas. Jung called the inner child puer aeternus (eternal boy) or puella aeterna (eternal girl).

Our inner child is, on one hand, a source of spontaneity, playfulness, and creativity, but it also has dark aspects manifesting as immaturity, dependence on others, insufficient self-love, low self-esteem, or inability to take responsibility for one's actions.

Jung did not directly use the term inner child in the sense we understand it today, but his theories about archetypes laid the foundation for modern understanding of this concept. The concept became more popular and understandable through the work of therapists and psychologists such as John Bradshaw, Alice Miller, and Eric Berne.

In the context of neuroscience, the inner child pertains to neural connections created and shaped during the first years of our lives. Neuroscientific findings show how our early experiences and childhood wounds can influence certain brain parts, including the limbic system, which plays a key role in regulating our emotions and social behavior.

If we pay careful attention to our behavior in conflict situations or when expectations are unmet, we can perceive recurring behavioral patterns. Often they are identical to behavior we observed in ourselves or our surroundings in childhood.

We must realize that the inner child within us does not age at the same rate as our body. People can have wrinkles and wealth of experience, but the inner child within them remains constantly present.

It is not only about speed, but also about how learning occurs. From birth to three, basic neural connections and basic social, emotional, and cognitive skills form most intensively.

Between three and six years old, neural connections become refined and expand; children develop more complex social abilities, emotional skills, and initial academic abilities. Simply put, during the first six years of life, children have minds like sponges—able to absorb enormous amounts of information.

At an early age, the human brain has great neuroplasticity, meaning it is extraordinarily capable of forming new neural connections. Availability heuristic is another cognitive distortion that can influence our judgment and shows that our mind has a natural tendency to favor information that we can easily recall—especially based on recent or emotionally vivid events, because people can remember these better.

The halo effect causes one characteristic to influence our overall perception of a specific person or object. When we perceive someone positively, we tend to attribute other positive qualities to them, such as intelligence, competence, or friendliness. The anchoring effect influences our mind so that when making decisions, we bounce off initial information. Hindsight bias in us evokes a feeling that events that occurred were more predictable than they actually were.

The Dunning-Kruger effect is a psychological phenomenon in which people with limited knowledge or abilities in a certain field tend to overestimate their knowledge of it.

The false consensus effect leads us to assume that our opinions and behavioral patterns are more common and more widely accepted in society than they actually are.

The false memory effect points to our tendency to incorrectly remember past events.

My Fear

Overcoming fear helps us think more rationally and utilize our brain more fully, which gradually leads to increased consciousness.

Fear is one of the oldest emotions. From an evolutionary perspective, it arose as an inseparable condition for life. In an environment where energy was scarce and every energy source was important for survival, the ability to quickly react to potential threats had crucial significance.

Primary forms of fear were a kind of signal whose purpose was to prevent energy loss and thus increase the chances of survival. This instinct activated defensive or escape mechanisms, such as fight, flight, or freeze responses, which helped individuals protect themselves from predators or other dangers. They evolved alongside more complex life forms, leading to the development of more sophisticated emotional states including fear as we know it today.

Our ancestors were often exposed to various dangerous situations, such as encounters with predators, passage through dangerous terrain, or attacks by enemy groups. Fear allowed them to more quickly recognize risks and adapt in order to survive and protect their offspring. Fear arouses a high degree of vigilance and increases physical readiness for danger, which had adaptive significance in the context of evolution.

In the modern world, fear is much less useful and can manifest as anxiety or phobia from situations that are not actually dangerous. These include fear of rejection, fear of the future, fear of job loss, fear of losing a partner, fear of illness, fear of loneliness, fear of enclosed spaces, fear of driving, fear of social interaction, fear of losing control, fear of failure, fear of responsibility, fear of failure or even fear of success and responsibility for it. People can acquire fear even of the color yellow (xanthophobia) or of string (linonophobia).

Whenever fear ceases to be real fear and becomes psychological, it is irrational fear driven by our ego. It is a complicated psychological mechanism of our "Primitive Self," which does not stem from real threat but is triggered by the voice in our head. That voice constantly repeats a certain threat, forcing us to experience unpleasant feelings over and over.

Our intellect can imagine countless ways danger could be even worse, or reasons why we should really be afraid. We can defend even completely irrational fear.

Fear is controlled by a part of the brain called the amygdala—the so-called threat center. It works complexly with other parts of the brain, but primarily maintains people's alertness and warns them of danger. When something threatens us, neurons in the amygdala activate. This allows us to identify threats and respond to them.

When we feel fear, our body releases adrenaline and other stress hormones, giving us the energy and strength needed to quickly escape a threatening situation. Our heart rate increases, breathing accelerates, and blood flow to muscles improves—all with the goal of preparing the body to fight or flee.

Fight-or-flight, that is \"fight or escape,\" is a typical fear response. In the past, it helped us survive in situations where life was truly threatened. Processes associated with this response were optimized by evolution and occur very quickly, automatically, and outside logical thinking. When we are afraid of something, logical thinking is pushed into the background. Under normal circumstances, we identify a situation in our environment through sensory perceptions and the brain decides how to handle it. If our mind identifies something as a threat, the entire process can occur outside logical thinking and we may not even realize what happened. This accelerated process can save us a second of hesitation, which in some situations can save our lives. Those who in the past could better respond to threats had a greater chance of survival. Those who survived had a greater chance of reproduction and passing on their genes. Descendants of such an individual inherited approximately the same good responses to threats.

Being afraid of something that threatens our life is completely fine. It is justified fear. Thanks to the heightened caution of our ancestors, we can read these lines. When we experience fear, our heart rate increases, blood vessels dilate, breathing accelerates, and glucose production increases. All these reactions make sense. We get more oxygen, our muscles become engorged with blood, and due to increased glucose levels, we have enough energy to fight or flee. From a psychological perspective, we will feel a sense of threat and anxiety. We will feel uncomfortable.

Unfortunately, people can be afraid of things that are not real and are only imagination in their head. These imaginations can sometimes literally paralyze us, even though they are absolutely improbable scenarios that we can, however, imagine as real. New brain structures can in such cases \"shut down,\" control is taken over by primitive brain structures, and mental paralysis, panic, and sometimes even paranoid states occur.

Since our brain cannot properly assess when it is a real threat to life, the above-mentioned physiological responses occur with any psychological fear—fear of failure, fear of losing a partner, fear of social condemnation, or even fear of uncertain future events.

When we think about it carefully, we come to realize that fear is always connected with the future. We cannot fear something that has already happened. Oh, I'm so afraid of what happened to me yesterday. That makes no sense. Over the past we can feel regret, guilt, or shame, but fear is always connected with the future.

Fear of potential future events arose in connection with higher cognitive thinking. Our mind evolved in such a way that we can perceive time, imagine possible scenarios, or predict the outcomes of future events, which often leads precisely to irrational and baseless fear. We can fear something we think might happen, even if the probability is almost zero.

We fear whatever we subjectively consider to be true. Without any evidence. Without any analysis. Sometimes one unverified piece of information is enough and people go crazy with fear, which then usually manifests in instinctive reactions. Our \"Primitive Self\" activates and we soon become capable of shouting, insulting, humiliating, cursing, physically harming, suspecting, threatening, belittling, or choosing another aggressive expression similar to a frightened animal. Yet in the vast majority of cases, we fear things that will never happen. We fear the imagination we created in our head. We fear something we heard on television. We fear something that makes no sense at all. Human imagination is completely sufficient to trigger all negative physiological manifestations.

Fear affects our body at the level of biological processes, changes our brain physiology, and influences our thinking and behavior. Psychological fear is often the result of our thought patterns, prejudices, and beliefs that may be completely separated from real circumstances. Reactions such as muscle engorging, increased heart rate, accelerated breathing, and feelings of anxiety, all based only on our subjective imagination, no longer make as much sense as in the case of real danger.

Fear is one of the most common and strongest emotions. Ultimately, all fear is primarily fear of death, which is associated with the basic survival instinct. We can observe it in everyday situations. We easily, for example, agree with senseless opinions in society because we are afraid that our different opinion will not be accepted. If we think about it carefully, what we are actually afraid of, we will find that even in this case it is fear of death, which is controlled by psychological processes of our \"Primitive Self.\"

Loneliness was in the past a great danger. An individual's survival in harsh nature was simpler within a group (family or tribe) and was also dependent on the ability to cooperate within it. Nowadays we are not dependent on the collective to the extent we were in the past, but this fear is deeply embedded and maintained in us. Another is anonymity, which reduces responsibility for individual acts, or suggestibility, through which a crowd is easily influenced by authorities and simple ideas. It is widely documented that Adolf Hitler allegedly drew and utilized knowledge from this book, which contributed to his rise and maintenance of power in Nazi Germany.

The basic mechanism of fear has its origin in our evolution and in harsh nature protected us from danger. There is nothing wrong with being afraid to pet a tiger or jump out of an airplane. Overcoming fear brings a person unimaginable benefits that improve their life in all directions. It even changes the physiology of our brain. Activity in the amygdala decreases and so does the production of the stress hormone cortisol, which causes a more relaxed and clearer state of consciousness. Increased activity in the prefrontal cortex, on the other hand, allows us to use higher cognitive abilities, make more logical decisions, plan better, focus better, and suppress unnecessary emotions. Serotonin levels also increase, which helps us feel better and happier.

Overcoming fear helps us function at a higher energy level, think more rationally, and utilize our brain more fully, which gradually leads to increased consciousness.

The Evolutionary Development of Emotions

Introduction

Our emotional states result from an unimaginably vast and complex evolutionary development. Primary emotions arose as tools helping us survive, orient ourselves in the world, and function in groups.

Later, higher emotions emerged, refining our capacity for cooperation and coexistence in larger communities.

A few thousand years ago, transcendent emotional states developed with the power to fundamentally transform not only an individual's life and surroundings, but the entire world. The ability to experience these highest states requires a certain degree of personal, psychological, and spiritual development that remains rare in the population.

Yet while still uncommon, they represent the next possible step in human evolution—a step that can transform not only how we experience our own lives, but also what kind of world we leave behind.

Let us examine how this development has shaped life on our planet to modern humanity and what it might tell us about the next step before us.

Overview of Emotions' Evolution and Their Contributions

The Evolution of Emotional Spectrum

Moving from lower emotions to higher ones is not only an evolutionary shift in consciousness, but also the key to the development of both the individual and society as a whole.

Recall that one of the most fundamental principles of every living organism on this planet is efficient energy management. From the molecular level to complex ecosystems, all living organisms must continuously optimize their energy processes in an ever-changing environment.

In the first part of this book, we examined the evolutionarily oldest emotions and their often negative impact on individual life. We have only briefly touched upon other layers of emotional experience. Now let us examine the entire emotional spectrum from a broader perspective to understand it in the context of evolutionary development. When we comprehend how and why individual emotions formed, many seemingly illogical reactions begin to make sense and human behavior ceases to appear chaotic.

At the same time, we understand why experiencing evolutionarily younger emotions positively affects not only individual life but also society's functioning as a whole.

Primary emotions arose as simple reaction mechanisms that helped living organisms increase energy, waste less, or spend it efficiently. When we examine emotions in the context of this basic biological principle, we realize emotions play a crucial role in the energy economy of living organisms—from individuals to entire societies.

The first reaction mechanisms helped organisms search for food, avoid danger, or find suitable mates. Initially, these were simple impulses and instinctive reactions, but as cognitive abilities developed and more complex social structures emerged, more intricate emotions followed—hierarchical emotions, social emotions, and higher emotions.

These more complex emotions enabled individuals to fit better within groups. They helped build social bonds, increase group stability, and enable entire groups to survive and prosper.

If we are consistent, we notice that younger emotions support cooperation or coexistence of increasingly larger wholes. The evolutionarily oldest emotions focused on individual survival. Younger ones supported survival and cooperation of same-species individuals within groups. The evolutionarily youngest emotions support cooperation and problem-solving in large communities up to the global level.

Only recently have "transcendent" emotions, or higher states of consciousness been added to our repertoire—states unknown to most of the population yet capable of transforming not only an individual's and entire nations' lives, but life itself.

The first records of transcendent emotional states date back only a few thousand years and provide valuable information about how our ancestors understood and explored profound states of consciousness. As mentioned, most of the population views transcendent emotional states as abstract and fails to understand their benefits. The average person cannot imagine how "higher" and "transcendent" emotional states could help in daily life.

By tracing the evolution of individual emotions, however, we can understand their true and profound meaning. Through this, we can better comprehend how fundamentally higher consciousness states can improve life quality, bring us inner peace, strengthen our personal and professional relationships, and open doors to unforeseen opportunities.

In the third part of this book, we will delve deeper into this topic to clarify the evolutionary significance and reveal the true potential of these "emotional" states. At this moment, it is important to note that advanced consciousness states bring enormous benefits not only to the individual in daily life but equally positively affect all forms of life on our planet, transcending the boundaries of time and space.

Our understanding of emotions' evolution is based on a combination of various scientific approaches. Comparative neurology, ethology, paleoneurobiology, evolutionary psychology, as well as genetic and molecular studies provide us a rich and complex picture of emotions' evolution and their significance in organisms' lives.

We must recognize that all emotions, including negative ones, have evolutionary meaning. The "more primitive" emotions—fear, cravings, aversion, and anger—arose to enable survival in harsh and hostile conditions. These emotions began appearing with the first nervous systems in simpler life forms approximately 600 million years ago.

Fear protects the organism from potential danger. Craving drives an individual toward acquiring resources or mating. Anger signals an unfulfilled craving, violation of territorial or social boundaries, but can also motivate change. These are basic forms of emotions or behavioral reactions connected to individual survival. As organisms became more complex, this led to more intricate nervous systems. Over hundreds of millions of years, their sensory capabilities, territorial defense, and energy and food-seeking abilities became increasingly refined.

During the period 600 to 300 million years ago, Earth underwent significant geological and biological changes that fundamentally influenced life's development. Basic emotions in vertebrates developed into a more sophisticated form approximately 300 million years ago. Organisms' curiosity and intelligence increased, leading to exploratory behavior and discovery of new resources and shelters.

Approximately 230 million years ago, with the first mammals, more complex brain structures developed, such as the midbrain and basal ganglia. These structures gave organisms better capacity to respond to environmental changes, more efficiently avoid predators, and hunt prey more rapidly. Importantly, they supported primitive social behavior among same-species individuals. In prehistoric times, many species did not show high levels of offspring care. Most ceased caring for their young after laying eggs. Young survival depended mainly on their ability to survive after hatching. However, as behavior complexity increased and evolutionary pressures mounted, some reptiles, fish, and birds developed care for eggs and offspring after hatching, which we can understand as the first manifestation of love and attachment between mother and young. This evolutionary progress is a fundamental building block for social bonds and further consciousness development. Emotions we consider "love" have roots in these basic parental bonds.

For some animal species, this bond was fundamental to survival and reproductive success. It was the first version of love, further developed especially in mammals, for which care of young is a main characteristic. Young mammals are fed with mother's milk after birth and typically need mother's protection and care.

More developed mammals began to "recognize" the advantages of expanded socialization and improved their social interactions. Increased social interaction and more complex social structures contributed to evolutionary pressure for developing more intricate brain structures, including the limbic system. This system is informally called the "mammalian brain" because it associates with functions typical of mammals and their capacity to build complex social relationships.

The evolutionary development of consciousness reflects a universal predisposition toward connection and unity across various spheres of being. Thus approximately 160 million years ago, mammals acquired more developed emotions such as shame, guilt, more complex aversion, jealousy, envy, joy, sadness, anxiety, and also attachment, pair bonding, and maternal love. The specific purpose of some of these emotions may not be as obvious as with primary emotions, but their evolutionary meaning is simple to understand.

For example, shame prompts an individual to reconsider their behavior within the group and possibly take corrective action. An individual feeling shame is more willing to back down, which can help prevent physical conflicts or social exclusion. Guilt acts as an internal behavior regulator, prompting correction of wrong actions and restoration of damaged social bonds, thereby improving group stability. Jealousy serves as an instinctive signal that a threat exists, motivating the individual to protect their resources, status, or partner. When an individual notices that someone else has something better, envy can act as motivation for improvement. Joy signals achievement of a goal or gain of advantage, which can motivate repetition of the same behavior. Conversely, sadness signals loss or failure, prompting reconsideration of behavior.

More complex manifestations of all these emotions can be observed in mammals with fully developed limbic systems, but their simple forms existed in nature hundreds of millions of years ago. In more complex social structures, expanded social and hierarchical emotions emerged such as pride, disgust, contempt, and anxiety, as well as life-supporting emotions like hope, loyalty, and enthusiasm. Each of these emotions provided important information about our position in the social hierarchy, about possible threats or opportunities. These emotions had high "information value" because they enabled us to understand our surroundings and make decisions that increased our chances of survival and success in the social environment.

The function of all evolutionarily older emotions is generally focused on survival, reproduction, or personal advantage of the individual. All are primarily products of older brain structures and part of the equipment of many animals from the simplest to the most developed, including Homo sapiens. For vast numbers of people, these are even predominant emotional states, despite our brains being supplemented with "newer" parts such as the neocortex or prefrontal cortex.

As mentioned, all evolutionarily older emotions are a double-edged sword in today's world because their significance had meaning only in specific contexts under harsh nature conditions. Emotions like fear, anger, or jealousy played a key role in survival in the past, but in today's world they are sources of psychological suffering, health problems, prevent personal growth, distort reality, and significantly contribute to interpersonal conflicts. If any of these "more primitive" emotions becomes our predominant emotional state, it creates an emotional attractor field that influences our life negatively (we will discuss emotional attractors in detail in the second part of this book). If someone is dominated by lower emotions, one could say that such an individual's emotional, psychological, and spiritual development is still at the beginning. In other words, their consciousness has not yet developed sufficiently to transcend their "Primitive Self."

Though within human evolution these emotions have their place and meaning, they often relate to reactions that are more instinctive than rational. On one hand, they may appear as a barrier, but can also serve as valuable feedback about our "poor" inner state. The more frequently we experience lower emotions, the more negatively they affect not only our life but also the lives of those around us.

In the context of overall consciousness development, we can consider lower emotions as indicators that a person is still in a process of maturation and self-knowledge. Expanding the spectrum of experienced emotions to include higher and transcendent ones, as well as the ability to constructively process the "lower" ones, is a sign of a higher degree of consciousness development.

Higher Emotions

The evolutionary development of emotions provides us not only with a view of our past, but also shows us the direction we should move toward.

In the early stages of evolution, emotions such as fear, cravings, aversion, and anger were important for individual survival. However, with the growth in complexity of social structures and the need for greater cooperation among individuals, higher emotions became key. Their significance in complex social structures is immense. Cooperation enables individuals to divide among themselves the energy, costs, and effort required to achieve a common goal. What would cost a single individual enormous effort and time, a cooperating collective can solve with considerably less effort.

Imagine one person trying to turn a giant wheel. The energy needed would be enormous if the individual were even capable of it. When other individuals join this task, however, they can turn the wheel with minimal effort. The result of a cooperating group's work often is incomparable to the work of an individual, especially when we consider aspects beyond the effort expended.

Living organisms evolved over hundreds of millions of years to perfect the capacity for cooperation and coexistence. Higher emotions enable us to cooperate, trust one another, and create stable family and social structures that are the foundation of modern societies. They enable us to create cooperating collectives capable of solving more complex problems than an individual could solve alone. Higher emotions have enabled us to cooperate in enormous groups, which positively impacts our daily life.

As another example, we can mention the construction of large structures such as bridges. Such a task is unrealizable for an individual. It is possible only through the combined effort of many specialists—architects, engineers, project managers, workers, electricians, transportation teams, safety teams, administrative workers, and other people who contribute to such a project. When the bridge is built, all individuals in the community benefit. Travel time shortens, access to resources and work opportunities improves, economic and social bonds between communities strengthen, new business opportunities emerge, access to educational facilities improves, and many other visible and less obvious advantages arise.

Evolutionarily younger emotions and attitudes, such as gratitude, compassion, empathy, humility, acceptance, reverence, willingness, enthusiasm, or love, help build large communities, increase the efficiency of communication, and support cooperation, which ultimately increases energy efficiency at the collective level.

Higher emotions evolved naturally and were guided by higher intelligence. They are evidence that the universe contains a natural tendency toward greater integration and unity at various levels of existence. These emotions began appearing approximately 2–3 million years ago along with the newest brain structure—the prefrontal cortex. This brain area participates in higher cognitive functions such as decision-making, planning, social behavior, self-awareness, or regulation of impulsive reactions. The prefrontal cortex enables great primates to perform complex tasks requiring planning and foresight, and also enables them to create various tools or recognize themselves in a mirror. However, the human prefrontal cortex is significantly more developed and sophisticated compared to other animals. Thanks to it, we can perform more complex cognitive operations and create deeper social interactions that lead to greater cooperation, stability, and harmony.

Evolutionary development therefore expanded our social behavior, enabled us to regulate impulsive emotional reactions, and supplemented our repertoire with higher emotions.

Of course, this development proceeded nonlinearly. It was not simply about adding "better" or "higher" emotions to the "lower" ones. Newer and older brain parts work together in a complex manner. For example, in situations where fear might be counterproductive, the prefrontal cortex modulates the reactions of the limbic system and we respond less impulsively. The newer brain structure regulates primitive, illogical, or aggressive behavior that we might expect from animals.

When we think more deeply, we can notice that higher emotions, such as empathy, compassion, maternal love, love for family, reverence, acceptance, and humility, help strengthen social bonds, reduce conflicts, increase communication efficiency, and maintain long-term relationships, which increases the chances of survival not only of the individual but of the entire society.

Empathy is extremely important so we can understand the feelings of others and better comprehend their perspective.

Compassion drives an individual toward supporting others in distress or suffering.

Maternal love instinctively leads a mother to protect and care for her children.

Love for family strengthens bonds between family members and close friends, thereby supporting cooperation and mutual support.

Reverence brings respect and admiration toward individuals based on their capabilities, accomplishments, or position.

Acceptance enables individuals to integrate into a group, thereby strengthening social bonds.

Humility enables an individual to recognize their own limitations, which supports personal development. It helps us respect others, thereby supporting harmony and cooperation in society.

Beyond this, higher emotions demonstrably affect physical, mental, and spiritual health in a positive way, increase creativity, improve cognitive capabilities, and overall energy efficiency of the individual.

People who have transcended their "Primitive Self" prefer precisely evolutionarily younger emotions, because they can understand their advantages and observe daily their positive impact on life. Lower emotions and attitudes, such as fear, distrust, jealousy, contempt, anger, guilt, or pride, affect an individual's life negatively or destructively. Higher emotions are beneficial for life, while lower emotions weaken life. Higher emotions move us forward, while lower emotions cause stagnation and decline.

If someone is dominated by higher emotions, one could say that such an individual's emotional, psychological, and spiritual development has progressed to a more advanced stage of consciousness development.

Experiencing higher emotions is not only a sign of a higher level of consciousness, but also a path toward achieving it. This progress represents a transition from a reactive to a proactive approach in our inner world. Rather than allowing lower emotions and automatic thinking patterns to determine our reactions, we actively choose higher emotions that are constructive, support our inner well-being, and personal development.

This process requires patience and self-awareness, but brings deeply transformational results not only for ourselves but also for our relationships. Higher emotions fundamentally change the way we perceive the world around us and how we influence one another.

Transcendent Emotions

While primitive emotions are deeply rooted in our evolutionary past, higher and transcendent emotions are key to the future and direction of human civilization.

Examining the overall development of emotions, we observe that only a few thousand years ago new "transcendent emotions" were added to our repertoire, allowing us to transcend individual existential experience and expand our perception. With transcendent emotions, we can no longer speak of emotions but rather of states of consciousness, since emotions are transitory and influenced by external stimuli. States of consciousness, conversely, are not transitory and are not influenced by external stimuli. More accurately, we call them enduring states of consciousness. These include unconditional love, deep compassion, heightened empathy, bliss, inner peace, or unity.

Although in evolutionary terms they are relatively new and not as widespread as primitive emotional states, this does not mean they are less real or less significant. The fact is that their spread among human beings is an important step in the evolution of human consciousness.

Transcendent emotional states are absolutely unknown to the majority of the population. The ability to experience them requires a certain degree of personal, psychological, and spiritual development, which is still very rare and in some people even provokes resistance. Therefore, it is not surprising that many people who do not yet have subjective experience with these evolutionarily newest emotional states do not understand them or do not recognize them as real.

A higher state of consciousness, such as unconditional love, bliss, or inner peace, is a deep and enduring feeling uninfluenced by external circumstances or situations. This state transcends basic instinctive or biological reactions and helps the individual enter a sphere of deep realization, reflecting the ability to connect with something greater. An individual in this state can more easily transcend natural limitations of mind, such as black-and-white perception (dualism), simplification through causal relationships (causality), or linear perception of the world—which we will explain in detail in coming chapters.

Evolutionarily, these states are newest and connected with deep introspection, radical self-reflection, expanded realization, and spiritual experience. Unlike ordinary emotions, transcendent states fundamentally transform not only individual lives but also their surroundings.

Despite the fact that a high state of consciousness is celebrated and supported in many cultures and religious traditions, in most modern Western cultures there is a strong emphasis on rational materialism, which holds completely different values—wealth, possessions, social status, career success, competitiveness, technological progress, practicality, usefulness, the number of followers on social media, the brand of one's car, one's place of residence, membership in certain clubs, or other superficial values. Attempting to explain to an ordinary modern person that we will not find lasting happiness and contentment in material values and social status will seem like madness. A description of high states of consciousness may sound interesting to such a person, but at the same time distant and incomprehensible.

In modern Western culture, high states of consciousness may be perceived with great skepticism or even understood as signs of mental illness. The truth, however, is that transcendent emotional states and higher states of consciousness are immensely complex subjective experiences that actually transcend the boundaries of verbal description. In their essence, they are indescribable. We cannot precisely define them, because they exist beyond the framework of rational reasoning and cannot be fully captured through language. It is simpler to explain what they are not than to attempt to explain what they are.

They are far richer and more complex than what can be fully comprehended through the mind. This is also one of the reasons why they do not have high priority for people. People simply do not understand the advantages that these states bring.

If, however, we wanted to approximately convey what a high state of consciousness provides to the modern person, one in which transcendent emotional states predominate, several irrefutable advantages could be mentioned.

Deep understanding, empathy, compassion, unconditional love, and respect for diversity enable individuals to significantly improve personal and professional relationships. This developed view minimizes interpersonal conflicts, improves cooperation, and leads to effective communication—with greater success in every area of life and incomparably less effort. A new way of existing, where we need not conflict, compete, fight, compare, gossip, or judge superficially, brings harmony with the outer world.

Outer harmony harmonizes our inner being, creating inner peace and minimizing negative emotions. Stress, anxiety, and other negative states decrease, fundamentally affecting physical and mental health. Constant judgment, commentary, and criticism of others create unconscious inner guilt, often leading to self-condemnation, punishment, harm, and self-hatred. Deeply, we know we lack understanding of connections to justify judging anyone, including ourselves.

A high state of consciousness literally heals us on all levels of our existence and overall leads to a more peaceful and balanced life. Since we are not in constant conflict with other people or with ourselves, our inner peace and harmony enable us to think highly creatively and even transrationally, but they also enable us to face life's challenges and changes with greater peace and resilience.

Compassion, empathy, love for life, and deeper understanding lead to meaningful and fulfilling existence, bringing continual joy and contentment. Higher consciousness encourages self-knowledge, self-reflection, and radical humility—key to growth and development. This liberates us from constant conflict with the world, from negative emotions, stress, anxiety, health problems, and mental fatigue.

The true scope and depth of these emotional states' influence on individuals and their perception is difficult to explain in words, given their subjective nature. Their influence is far more complex, and therefore we examine transcendent emotional states and high consciousness in the next part from various perspectives.

The first records of transcendent emotions, or high states of consciousness, reach back approximately 4-5 thousand years into the past, although exact estimates may vary depending on the source and interpretation of archaeological findings.

From history we know that approximately 2-3 thousand years before the common era, Krishna, a key figure in Hindu culture, taught about unconditional love known as bhakti. His teachings spread throughout India and dealt with other transcendent topics and advanced states of consciousness.

Written references to high states of consciousness can be found, for example, in the Hindu sacred text Bhagavad Gita, which speaks of states of unconditional love and inner peace.

Unconditional love is equally important in Buddhist teaching as well. Within it, one can encounter the term metta, which is often translated as loving-kindness or unconditional love, and according to Buddhist teaching is one of the four ideal forms of human conduct. It is a form of love that is not conditioned by personal interest or attraction, and is extended equally to all beings.

Further records of high states of consciousness are found in the ancient Chinese Daoist text Dao De Jing, which promotes life in harmony and the seeking of inner peace through "merging with the natural order of the universe." This merger can be explained to a modern person of Western culture as a learning process in which we are in alignment with natural laws and universal truths, while we abandon the compulsive need to control every aspect of life. It is about finding balance between our personal goals and the natural flow of life.

Jesus Christ's teachings on unconditional and non-selective love left a deep impression on cultural and philosophical traditions. This topic is central to his teachings and thoroughly documented in the New Testament. Regardless of religious context, his teachings inspired altruism, influenced non-violent movements leading to positive social change, and brought universal principles impacting humanity.

The concept of unconditional love, which is present in many spiritual teachings around the world, has survived millennia and positively influences billions of people. Although skeptics and critics may question these teachings or consider them outdated, we cannot ignore the ever-present evolutionary tendency of our emotions to move toward higher and transcendent states. In spite of differences in interpretations and practices, it is clear that the movement toward unconditional love and other transcendent emotions is a result of the natural development of human psychology and a part of our collective desire for greater understanding, greater harmony, and unity. To be in conflict with this natural development is like being in conflict with evolution itself.

Throughout history, individuals from various corners of the world have sought to spread their deep and timeless knowledge of high states of consciousness. They emphasized the value and power of transcendent emotional states—unconditional love, bliss, deep empathy, inner peace, unity—teaching that such states fundamentally change both individual inner worlds and the world around us.

Among the most well-known were, for example, Siddhartha Gautama, the founder of Buddhism, better known as Buddha, who taught about unconditional love and compassion as an important aspect of enlightenment, or Jesus Christ, who taught about love for one's neighbor as oneself and also emphasized unconditional and non-selective love.

Another significant figure who spread the concept of unconditional love was a leader in the field of consciousness, renowned psychiatrist, scientist, and spiritual teacher David R. Hawkins, who emphasized that unconditional love is one of the highest forms of human consciousness. Similarly, Agnes Gonxhe Bojaxhiu, better known as Mother Teresa, who devoted her life to serving the poor and the sick, also frequently spoke about the importance of unconditional love.

Martin Luther King Jr., a leader of the civil rights movement in the United States, taught about the power of unconditional love in the struggle against injustice. Mohandas Gandhi, who was known in India for his non-violence movement (ahimsa) and kindness toward all living beings, was a significant promoter of unconditional love and called for understanding among different nations, religions, and cultures. Nelson Mandela, a significant South African politician and activist, emphasized the importance of unconditional love and reconciliation toward his enemies as a means of overcoming racial segregation and discrimination.

The Tibetan philosopher and spiritual leader of Tibetan Buddhism and current Dalai Lama Tenzin Gyatso also frequently speaks about the importance of compassion and unconditional love toward all living beings.

These and other teachers with high consciousness have taught the incredible impact of unconditional love and other transcendent emotions. Since these relatively new "emotional states" are difficult to understand logically, most people struggle to accept them. Today, however, we can demonstrably prove the benefits of unconditional love not only as abstract spiritual concept but also as concrete benefits.

In the psychological context, unconditional love reduces anxiety, depression, low emotional states, and improves overall mental well-being.

In the field of neuroscience, it has been found that higher emotional states affect the physiology of the brain, specifically the levels of dopamine and serotonin, which leads to reduced stress, a higher level of happiness, and overall well-being. Serotonin also affects the levels of melatonin, which regulates the sleep cycle, leading to better sleep.

From the perspective of physical health, people who view the world through the eyes of unconditional love have a strengthened immune system, are less prone to various diseases, and have a longer average lifespan.

In interpersonal relationships, unconditional love creates deeper and more stable bonds, reduces conflicts, and promotes healthy social interaction, which ultimately leads to stronger and lasting relationships.

In the work environment, it is able to improve team cooperation and increase overall productivity.

At the level of society, it supports tolerance, acceptance of diversity, and cohesion, thereby helping to reduce social tension and conflicts and increasing overall stability in society.

These are only some of the added values that higher and transcendent emotional states, such as unconditional love, bring. Overall, this is a multitude of tangible advantages that can be supported by evidence and that support not only individual but also collective development.

Many people are raised in an environment where logical and analytical thinking dominates, and therefore it can be difficult for them to truly understand or experience higher states of consciousness. Furthermore, society tends to evaluate success based on the already-mentioned external measures, such as wealth, possessions, social status, the number of followers on social media, the brand of one's car, one's place of residence, and other superficial values that can be placed in some table, compared with each other, and assigned a number.

The mind, however, cannot evaluate the degree of inner peace, contentment, love, inner tranquility, or harmony with the environment. Therefore, transcendent emotional states and high states of consciousness tend to be perceived as less important or even pointless. In people with low consciousness, they even appear as weakness.

Let us remind ourselves that individuals with a low level of consciousness, where primitive mechanisms predominate, focus mainly on their immediate experience and personal needs. In such a state, empathy, humility, or love is perceived as weakness or danger, because these qualities often require setting one's own needs aside.

As human consciousness develops, there is a gradual shift from primitive and instinctive reactions toward higher forms of emotional experience and interaction with people. Humility, understanding, and love are highly valued at a more advanced level of consciousness and are key to maintaining harmonious and functional personal and professional relationships.

Another reason why a higher state of consciousness, such as unconditional love, makes no sense to our mind is its impracticality. It simply seems illogical to her to devote time and energy to another human being without a return of this "investment." The mind can acknowledge that it is a "nice idea," but prefers to focus on what is tangible and measurable.

Striving for deep understanding, deep empathy, or indeed feeling love toward an unknown person on the other side of the planet makes no logical sense at first glance. Undeveloped consciousness will tell itself that trying for something like that makes sense at most for one's loved ones, but certainly not for unknown people. The "Primitive Self" will even consider such emotional states ridiculous, foolish, and dangerous.

Transcendent emotions, however, are far from foolish or dangerous. In the modern world, where communication and interaction at the global level are faster and more accessible, higher and transcendent emotional states are more important than ever before. They enable people to bridge cultural, racial, and religious differences and thus work together on solving global problems. While primitive emotions are deeply rooted in our evolutionary past, higher and transcendent emotions are key to the future and direction of human civilization. We can consider unconditional love as one of the pinnacles of the evolutionary development of consciousness. Its positive impact on the life of the individual and on society as a whole is invaluable.

The evolution of emotions offers us a fascinating perspective not only on our past but also on the direction we are heading. Through higher emotions, we are able to cooperate and achieve results that would be impossible for an individual.

Observing humanity's development, we notice we began cooperating in small groups, then large communities, and today function in globally interconnected communities through deeper understanding, compassion, empathy, and love. Evolutionary development shows the direction we should move. Transcendent emotions and consciousness development suggest that existing in harmonious wholeness benefits not only individuals but all humanity. This extends beyond the physical or material plane to all human existence, affecting all living organisms and planetary systems, showing the universe's natural tendency toward integration and unity across spheres of being.

This principle reflects the thoughts of many spiritual teachings and the knowledge that the most developed beings shared with us thousands of years ago.

The transition to the dominant experience of transcendent emotional states and high states of consciousness is often limited not only by the primitive mechanisms of our old brain structures or low emotional states but also by more sophisticated mechanisms connected to newer brain structures. In other words, the shift in the development of our consciousness is often hindered by our intellect.

We develop it throughout our entire life until we discover that for our further development, it becomes the main obstacle.

To transition from lower to higher emotional states and ultimately to the highest states of consciousness, it is essential to overcome the limitations of our own mind, which naturally operates within the frameworks of dualism, causality, linear perception, and is influenced by countless cognitive distortions. These limitations prevent us from fully understanding and experiencing higher and transcendent emotional states and from more deeply comprehending reality.
