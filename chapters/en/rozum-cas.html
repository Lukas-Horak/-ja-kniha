Time helps us structure and organize our daily activities. In interaction with other people or when performing routine life activities, it usually plays a significant role. The ability to perceive time helped individuals in the animal world increase their chances of survival because it allowed them to better perceive their environment, remember potential threats, and predict the movement of predators or prey.

After our ancestors realized the repeating movement of the Sun, the motion of oceans, or our planet, they began recording time. It was the rotation of Earth that became the basis for measuring time.

Through time we can measure and classify various events, compare their durations, and determine the speed of changes. We are also governed by time when planning work, meals, sleep, and performing daily duties.

Our collective ability to perceive time is also closely connected to the concept of intersubjectivity. However, we would have difficulty explaining to an ape that if it stacks bricks all day, we will give it bananas in the evening. Apes, like other animals, have limited cognitive abilities and are primarily driven by immediate needs and stimuli. They do not have a developed ability to understand abstract concepts such as time or future events. Animals live in the present moment. We humans assume that our perception of time is actual reality. However, this is not true. The universe does not care how people on the planet Earth perceive time.

Our perception of time is largely the result of biological and psychological processes that allow us to function efficiently in the physical world. The universe has no preferred perception of time. According to the theory of relativity developed by Albert Einstein, time and space blend and create a four-dimensional continuum called spacetime. Within spacetime, time can move at different speeds depending on the strength of gravity or the speed of movement. This means that what we perceive as the same moment in time may actually be a different time for another observer under different conditions.

Albert Einstein once wrote: "People like us who believe in physics know that the difference between past, present, and future is only a stubborn illusion." Some physicists have aligned with Einstein and agree that true reality is timeless or atemporal. This same view of reality is also part of many spiritual teachings. At the deepest level of existence, everything is interconnected and unified. Concepts like time and space are merely relative and limited ways that the human brain interprets and organizes experiences.

Moving Beyond Causality

Everything we experience are actually independent moments that emerge from the infinite whole and again merge with it.

Our intellect focuses on perceiving a linearly ordered sequence of events directed from past to future. This subjective experience of time is generated and measured by the activity of neurons in our brain.

The human mind has a natural tendency to connect events, simplify them, and see causal relationships between them. The mind incorrectly assumes that if a second event follows the first, it was "logically" caused by the previous event. This caused that. Cause and effect.

We explain almost everything around us in various forms of causal relationships we call causality. This is a natural deception of the mind. This way of perceiving prevents us from understanding a broader perspective because our intellect simply cannot comprehend the unity of the universe and limits itself to linear and causal perception of reality. However, without neurons creating imaginary connections of our subjective experiences stored in the past, there is no actual past. Without neurons creating ideas about potential future, there is no future. The only reality that is true is the present. It is the only moment we can subjectively experience. Each such moment is part of an infinite whole of moments from which our brain selected specific events and arranged them on an illusory timeline.

However, events in the universe lie outside the range of our linear perception. Just as our senses, reason allows us to perceive only a fraction of entire reality. Our attempts to describe reality do not describe actual reality but only our perception.

The concept of causality or the relationship between cause and effect has a long history. The oldest recorded thoughts on causality can be found in ancient Greek philosophy. In physics, causality is closely related to the concept of determinism, which holds that the future state of a physical system can be predicted with complete accuracy if the initial conditions are known. This idea is the basis of Newtonian physics and roughly states that events simply happen one after another. Everything has a cause and effect.

For the ordinary human mind, describing how the world works through Newtonian physics is sufficient. People naturally tend to perceive the world around them in relation to cause and effect. The ability to perceive causality is even considered a basic property of human thinking.

Studies in cognitive psychology have shown that people have a strong tendency to perceive causal relationships even when none exist. We simply imagine what caused what and consider it reality.

Imagine we hear a loud shot. It could be a backfire from a car's exhaust, a loud closing of a door, but it could also be a gunshot. Our brain does not know what causes this shot, it only knows about the effect, so it must guess. It enters rapid situation evaluation mode, relying on previous experiences and patterns. For example, if we have experienced unpleasant events related to gunshots in the past, our brain may be prone to respond to this sound with a higher level of vigilance or even paralyzing fear. On the other hand, if our previous experiences with similar sounds were mostly harmless (for example, car exhaust backfire), our brain would probably evaluate the situation as less threatening.

The entire process of guessing the cause is strongly influenced by our subjective experiences, expectations, and our perception. Not only do we often imagine what reality is, but this imagined reality differs from person to person.

Evolutionarily, understanding the relationship between cause and effect helped us better understand events in the physical world as well as predict future events. The ability to identify cause and effect was important for survival. For example, if our ancestors could recognize that consuming a certain plant (cause) caused illness (effect), it helped them survive. Causality thus helps us create models of the world we use to orient, plan, and decide.

However, the functioning of the universe and our everyday experiences cannot be described by simple deterministic laws. Determinism, causality, nor linear flow of time describe physical reality as the human mind mistakenly assumes. Newton's laws work very well under ordinary conditions, such as the motion of planets, cars, or falling apples. They tend to be useful in the everyday world, but for explaining broader reality they are extremely simplified. Exceptional situations such as proximity to a very strong gravitational field or motion close to the speed of light require different theories to describe these phenomena.

Newton's laws do not work in the world of subatomic particles either. The quantum universe is fundamentally probabilistic, as opposed to the deterministic universe described by Newtonian physics. If we begin to deal with mental processes, immaterial levels of reality, energy fields, or the influence of human consciousness on the material world, we cannot even consider Newtonian physics. In such cases, we move into nonlinear areas where it is necessary to perceive the universe as one whole of many interconnected parts.

Life and everyday events must be understood as a network of interconnected parts, where a change in one part can have unimaginably significant impact on the whole.

An amazing way of looking at the universe was provided to us by significant quantum physicist David Bohm. He argued that the entire universe is contained in each of its parts. Similarly, all time is contained in each flowing moment. Bohm showed us that at the subatomic level we are not actually separate from everything. In quantum reality, separation simply does not exist.

This view of the universe is based on the phenomenon of quantum entanglement, which suggests that particles can be connected regardless of the distance between them. As a result, changes in one particle can immediately manifest in another particle, regardless of their distance. The boundaries and separation we perceive in our everyday lives are only illusions at the macroscopic level. At the subatomic level, everything is connected and unity is the fundamental principle of the universe. Everything is part of one system.

Bohm sensed the completeness of the universe and provided us a different view of how the universe, time, and our place in it can be understood. According to his description, each moment is a projection of the whole. This moment is then projected back into the whole. Another moment again emerges from the whole, but includes the previous projection of the whole—and this repeats infinitely. The causality that occurs is not local, isolated, or separated from the whole.

The following analogy could serve for better understanding: Let us imagine individual moments in our lives as waves on an ocean. Each wave emerges from a huge ocean, for a brief moment is displayed in full beauty and then sinks back into the entire ocean. Another wave emerges similarly from the entire ocean. However, it is influenced by previous waves because they all emerge from the entire ocean and sink back into it. It is not that wave A creates wave B. It is that wave A influences wave B by being absorbed back into the ocean's whole, from which wave B then arises. Each wave is similar to previous waves but also differs in certain aspects, such as exact size, shape, duration, and so on. A wave is ultimately only a temporary expression of the ocean. It is a nonlocal phenomenon because what happens in any part of the ocean affects all its other parts.

From a certain perspective, waves appear as separate and distinct entities, but when we look closely, we see that all are part of a larger, connected whole. Similarly, individual elements of the universe may seem separate, but all are part of the same fundamental reality.

Everything we experience are actually independent moments that emerge from the infinite whole and again merge with it. Only our mind connects them into sequential events that it considers separate and isolated from the whole. The intellect connects these events in a limited, linear, and causal way.

Just as each wave may seem separate and distinct but is ultimately only a temporary expression of the ocean, individual moments in our lives may seem separate but are all part of a greater whole. The mind tells itself that moment A caused moment B. One event caused another. Cause and effect. This happened first and this second. The result is a specific moment in our life that we might judge as "right" or "wrong."

If we looked at this specific moment from a broader perspective, we would discover that it was part of a larger whole, thanks to which we managed to become a better, more experienced, or happier person. This specific moment was an integral part of our evolution and at the same time was part of a huge web of other moments. Each further distance would allow us to see an increasingly larger whole.

If we looked at an even broader timeline, we would discover that this moment completely disappears because it is part of an infinite number of other moments with countless connections. It would be only a fleeting glimpse in the development of a specific animal species, total life on the planet, formation of a galaxy, or the entire universe. It would not be possible to evaluate or judge the correctness or incorrectness of that moment because it was part of the infinite whole from which it arose and to which it returned.

Each moment in our lives is part of other connected moments that emerge from an infinite ocean of moments and sink back into this infinite ocean. It is a nonlocal and nonlinear phenomenon because what happens in any part of the ocean affects all its other parts.

The universe allows us for some reason to observe causal relationships. This does not mean our perception and understanding of the world is completely inaccurate. However, we must realize that it is a fraction of actual reality. The infinite and unlimited timelessness of the universe is indescribable. Events in our lives are connected in a way that our intellect cannot piece together. We are affected by nonlinear events that are located in spacetime "far apart" and appear to be completely unrelated. The ordinary mind cannot imagine how these events could be related and does not understand the forces that connect them.

Our mind is trained from childhood to this simplified perception of reality, so we consider causality as absolute reality. The event we perceive as cause comes before the event we perceive as effect. We seemingly perceive all necessary causal relationships, but actually we automatically create the specific causal relationship in our minds. Sometimes purely out of habit. Other times we primitively simplify it with "mental shortcuts" to save mental energy. Recall that our brain tries to save energy at every moment or work with it efficiently.

If we want to better understand the events happening in our lives, we need to look at them from a broader perspective and not limit our perception to only selected causal relationships. Causality is a concept created by the human mind and superficially helps us navigate better in the huge ocean of the universe. By moving beyond the boundaries of simple causal relationships and limitations of rational reasoning, we bring more understanding and peace to our lives. We realize that events in our lives are connected in an infinite whole. Moment by moment. We understand that everything is at the right time in the right place. At this moment we are located exactly where we should be. Every electron exists exactly where it should be, and the universe as such is exactly as it should be.

Accepting this perspective means recognizing that resistance to reality is unnecessary and limiting. Everything happens exactly as it should. When we realize this, much of our suffering disappears in a second. It is childish to get upset that something did not develop the way we projected in our heads. Once we place individual life moments into a broader perspective, our limited judgments, regret of specific situations, disagreement with them, or other evaluations of them completely disappear.

Beyond Linear Thinking

Attempting to understand life through linear thinking is like trying to solve a Rubik's cube by looking at only one side.

Nonlinear thinking allows us to see beyond the boundaries of linear cause-and-effect relationships. It helps us move beyond our ordinary thinking and recognize connections we previously could not perceive.

By transcending the limits of linear thinking, we begin to see patterns and connections between seemingly unrelated ideas, concepts, problems, and events. It helps us understand things that linear-thinking minds cannot grasp. Some things exist entirely outside the sphere of rational reasoning, making linearity irrelevant. They cannot be understood through logical thinking or intellectual analysis because they require direct experience, which is essential for deeper comprehension.

One such example is intuition. We must clarify at the outset that intuition is not the same as emotions, though people often confuse them. Our emotions are psychophysiological reactions to past experiences, usually linked to strong physical responses—a racing heart, tightened stomach, tension, or anxiety. Emotions are typically triggered by our past experiences, unhealed wounds, fears, or traumatic events, and they activate very quickly and unconsciously. They tell us nothing about present reality but rather about unresolved matters from the past. They originate from the "Primitive Self," are often dramatic, contradictory, and change day to day.

Intuition, by contrast, is quiet, clear, and produces no physical sensations. It is inner guidance that cannot be explained by logic but arrives with certainty. Often we say we know something without knowing how we know it. We are not speaking of expert intuition, developed through extensive training and practice in a specific field, which allows a person to quickly and almost effortlessly recognize patterns and make accurate judgments. We speak of moments of knowing that cannot be explained rationally and do not stem from broad experience.

Intuition is a valuable source of guidance we can attribute to the "Higher Self." Though this phenomenon is not yet scientifically fully explained, it involves a combination of observing nonverbal signals, empathy, and receiving information from collective consciousness.

Emotions represent another area whose complete understanding requires direct experience. We can understand emotions to a degree, yet our rational thinking cannot fully describe or comprehend them. They are internal experiences that cannot be captured in words. For example, genuine love represents a deep emotional state that transcends the boundaries of traditional logic and rational thinking. Although we cannot "logically" prove that we love someone, our actions and decisions often reveal the power and truth of our feelings. An extreme example is the willingness to sacrifice one's life for a loved one. This act is the ultimate expression of love and sacrifice, transcending the bounds of reason. Less extreme examples include selfless kindness toward strangers, which makes no sense to the ordinary mind.

Art is another area that cannot be explained or comprehended in detail by the mind. To fully understand a work of art requires subjective experience. Art is the product of human creativity and imagination existing outside linear thinking. It triggers reactions within us that are difficult to express in words. Although we can partially analyze art and describe its technical aspects, the true impact of a work can only be fully experienced through direct interaction with it. One painting can evoke different emotions in different people, just as a song can have different meanings for different listeners.

Attempting to understand a song through linear thinking is like trying to comprehend its melody by analyzing each individual note without ever actually hearing the full song. It would be the same if we tried to understand a joke by analyzing each word separately. For a joke to be funny, it depends on a specific combination of words, intonation, and timing of delivery, just as a song depends on a combination of lyrics, melody, rhythm, and emotion to create a powerful and often indescribable experience. Analyzing individual parts separately, isolated from the rest, cannot capture the essence or meaning of a song—nor can linear analysis of each word of a joke tell us anything about it. Thus, attempting to understand a song with the mind could be considered somewhat funny or completely pointless. Trying to describe a specific song to someone without them hearing it would be a comedy.

Imagine someone wanting to explain the musical experience of "Smells Like Teen Spirit" by Nirvana:

"This song begins with an energetic rock riff, like an approaching storm," Martin says enthusiastically.

"A storm? What do you mean? Do you mean the rock riff like 'Smoke On The Water' by Deep Purple?" Veronica asks.

"No, this is completely different. It's more like a revolutionary cry," Martin replies.

"A cry? Do you mean an outburst like Axl Rose's in 'Welcome to the Jungle'?" Veronica inquires.

"No, not at all! It's a completely different type of cry. And yes, it has mysterious lyrics: a mulatto, an albino, a mosquito, my libido," Martin continues.

"Wouldn't you rather just play it for me?" Veronica asks, frustrated.

And thus ends Martin's futile attempt to communicate a musical experience that requires above all personal encounter and inner experience, much like countless other ordinary and even transformative life experiences.

There are various experiences that cannot be explained through rational thinking, and the attempt to describe these experiences in words completely fails. Certain aspects of reality exist in a nonlinear sphere, and to understand them we must transcend rational thinking.

This sphere includes areas we will explore in later sections of this book—for example, higher and even transcendent emotional states, a feeling of unity with the universe, or deep spiritual awakening exist completely outside our mind's framework. These experiences are profoundly transformative and can fundamentally change our lives for the better, yet they often resist rational explanation and logical description. Similarly, it is impossible to fully understand or adequately describe in words the well-known feeling of love, the beauty of nature, or a simple sunset.

Although the mind is a remarkable tool, certain things lie beyond its reach. These experiences and states often cannot be reduced to simple verbal descriptions and are extremely difficult to explain adequately. They require direct experience, without which they may seem confusing. It is similar to trying to explain what sweetness tastes like to someone. We can describe it or use analogies, but words without direct experience ultimately lead nowhere. Sweet taste is simply sweet taste. Explanation without experience is impossible. True understanding comes only at the moment when a person tastes it themselves.

The ordinary human mind perceives the world linearly and prefers linear thinking. This does not mean people never use nonlinear thinking. Rather, linear thinking predominates. For most people, it is a comfortable and efficient way to approach problems and life situations. Linear thinking could be called the basic or default mode of the human mind. Nonlinear thinking emerges only when our consciousness begins to expand beyond the boundaries of rational thinking.

Linear thinking is more common among individuals strongly influenced by traditional educational systems. These often emphasize linear approaches, place great weight on memorizing content, and teach established methods for problem-solving. Traditional educational systems lead people to develop strong linear thinking skills but provide little opportunity to develop nonlinear thinking.

Linear thinking also characterizes those who prefer a high degree of predictability or control. This way of thinking helps people simplify complex situations and provides them a false sense of stability and security.

In linear thinking, our intellect takes pieces, arranges them in logical order, and then draws conclusions. The "Rational Self" tends to prefer a more structured and organized view of the world and approaches problems in a straightforward manner. Nonlinear events have no structure, and our mind perceives them as unpredictable chaos, leading to confusion and uncertainty.

Linear thinkers imagine planning their future like a drive down a straight highway. They estimate fuel consumption in advance, calculate speed, plan stops, and account for a few variables so they know exactly when and where they will arrive. In reality, life is more like a wild roller coaster with random circular twists, steep climbs and sharp drops, all poorly mapped on an amusement park guide we received at the entrance.

Linear planning gives people the feeling that they have everything "under control." Of course, this is merely an illusion, because none of us can control every variable in the nonlinear equation called life. This simplified view of the world has its roots in the "Primitive Self," stemming from fear and the desire to control uncontrollable aspects of life, which inevitably leads to negative emotions such as fear, sadness, regret, guilt, shame, uncertainty, and anger.

While linear thinkers are often surprised, frustrated, disappointed, and sometimes even paralyzed by common life events, nonlinear thinkers understand that life is full of variable factors. At higher levels of consciousness, nonlinear reality is accepted without resistance. The higher mind is not confined to linear and causal perception but rather expects unexpected turns and is prepared to adapt to new situations. Instead of trying to control every small detail, such individuals work within a broader framework and adapt as situations unfold.

Nonlinear thinkers not only think outside the box but can remove the box entirely. Their thinking is often more flexible and is not bound by traditional ways of thinking. Nonlinear thinking regularly produces "leaps in logic" and leads to more creative problem-solving.

The sooner we realize that everything in our life stems from nonlinear reality, the sooner we escape the web of unnecessary suffering. The key is to open ourselves to a new perspective that challenges our current beliefs, opinions, and assumptions. We must accept that there are things we do not know or do not understand, let go of the need to control everything, and make peace with uncertainty.

We need not take a seminar or pay membership fees to a nonlinear thinking club. It is purely a matter of inner realization.

Linear and nonlinear thinking are not opposites but perfectly complementary pairs. A person can use a combination of both styles in different situations.

Nonlinear thinking is more open, intuitive, and creative, allowing us to see apparently nonexistent relationships and achieve unexpected results. In higher states of consciousness, we naturally perceive reality as a dynamic and interconnected whole that we attempt to sense and experience from our own being rather than analyze with the mind, describe in words, or endlessly dissect through logical reasoning.

Linear thinking is based on sequential, logical, and analytical approaches through which a person tries to perceive and solve problems as isolated and separate from the whole. One might say that linear thinkers cannot see the forest for the trees.

Of course, there are situations where no significant nonlinear relationships affect the outcome and linear thinking with causality is entirely sufficient. An example is baking a cake from a recipe, where following each step precisely and using specific quantities of ingredients yields a predictable result.

Attempting to understand life and various life situations through linear thinking, however, is like trying to solve a Rubik's cube by looking at only one side. The view from one side may seem adequate, but the moment we try to move further, our entire plan falls apart and we remain confused and frustrated.

Like a Rubik's cube, life is a complex and multidimensional puzzle requiring a more holistic approach. People dominated by linear thinking cannot imagine how certain events could be related and do not understand the forces connecting them. They question, ignore, or view nonlinear relationships and nonlinear changes with great distrust because they are unpredictable and uncontrollable.

Yet we must understand that nonlinear changes are completely natural and occur in every area of our lives, including our work careers, interpersonal relationships, our health, and our behavior.

Accepting Nonlinearity

Life operates on complex principles, where small changes can significantly influence the larger whole.

We are increasingly aware that the universe is a complex and interconnected system influenced by countless factors, connections, and events that can trigger a domino effect at any moment.

Recall that we are not separate from the universe but are an inseparable part of it. This is the well-known "placebo effect," which is well documented in thousands of clinical studies. Put simply, our mind cannot fully distinguish between what is a mental projection and what is physical reality. Therefore, through mental projections, we can influence our body in a relatively simple manner.

If, for example, we imagine cutting an orange and tasting its sweet segments, an image of this reality appears in our mind. Our saliva begins to flow and our stomach prepares itself for digestion. Our body thinks the orange is real. What is called the insula is activated, which is connected to sensory experiences and the perception of the body, and can trigger reactions such as increased salivation or the feeling of hunger.

Another, less mentioned example is erotic fantasies, which trigger sexual arousal in the body. A simple thought activates various areas of the brain that work to make our imagination begin to materialize. The prefrontal cortex, the occipital lobe, the temporal lobe, the parietal lobe, as well as various areas of the limbic system coordinate to participate in the inner experience that can trigger physical reactions in the body.

Our brain in some cases does not fully know the difference between what "is real" and what "is not real." Immaterial thoughts can thus produce very material results. Thoughts can activate the same neurological pathways as real sensory experiences, which ultimately can affect our health, our perception of reality, and the overall quality of our lives. Research in neuroscience shows that imagining a certain life experience can activate similar brain regions as actually experiencing it. When, for example, a pianist imagines playing the piano, the same motor areas of the brain activate as when actually playing. In visualizing athletic performance, we engage the same neurological pathways as those we use in real training. When someone recalls a traumatic memory, the brain responds very similarly to how it would if the situation were happening again. Our thoughts and mental images not only influence how we perceive reality, but also trigger various physiological processes within us (from heart rate through stress hormone levels to immune system function). In other words, the contents of our mind can affect our organism as concretely as external experiences.

To avoid being automatically controlled by our own mental processes, we must learn to step outside our mind and realize that thoughts are merely transitory content, not the very essence of who we are. This capacity is naturally available at a higher level of consciousness. It allows us to transcend the mind and its contents. At this level of consciousness, we cease to be fully absorbed in the constant stream of thoughts, emotions, beliefs, social expectations, and sensory perceptions that dominate our attention in ordinary states of consciousness. What we considered to be our "self" (our mind) transforms into a tool we use to achieve goals and perform everyday activities. Rather than identifying with our mind and its contents, it becomes more of a tool we use in daily life. The mind is like a river that constantly flows and carries various thoughts with it. If we are trapped in it, its current carries us away and we often lose control. We cannot stop the river. However, if we step onto the shore, we can calmly observe it from a safe distance.

A higher level of consciousness allows us to transcend information from sensory data and the automatically generated contents of the mind, thereby changing not only the perception of ourselves but also our interaction with the surrounding world. Through this capacity, we can more easily recognize low emotional states, negative thoughts, limiting beliefs, and work with them constructively. In lower states of consciousness, this type of self-awareness is very limited or completely unavailable, so people remain trapped in their thoughts, feelings, and emotions. Their inner world defines what they observe and experience in the outer world. If their inner world is full of fear, the outer world appears to them as a hostile place. Everywhere they seek threats. If it is full of desires, something is always missing to them—whether it is recognition, attention, fame, praise, possessions, money, or success. If it is driven by anger, they see the world as unjust and hostile. They constantly struggle with someone, prove their truth, or blame someone, which only deepens their dissatisfaction and tension. They do not realize that the prison from which they are trying to escape is created by themselves in their own mind.

We are the directors of our own scenes, which we project in our minds and which subsequently become the real scenes of our everyday lives. By repeating certain thoughts, our neural networks strengthen—without our having control or conscious thought about it.

These activities occur in the unconscious part of the brain, which is important in regulating vital life processes such as the beating of the heart, breathing, blinking, or wound healing. We could not consciously devote constant attention to them; it would be too demanding and energetically costly. The universe has ensured that these activities function without our conscious thinking. Let us pause for a moment at this thought.

If our neural system can manage to regulate so many processes automatically, what possibilities open up for us when we begin to communicate with it consciously? Research confirms that when we devote ourselves to the conscious development of certain thoughts or emotional states, we can influence changes in brain structure and brain functions. The concept of consciously developing thoughts and states with the aim of influencing brain structures and functions is scientifically grounded. This process is known as conscious neuroplasticity. It stems from scientific knowledge that the brain is plastic and can change throughout life, meaning these changes are not limited to early childhood as was long assumed.

One of the key studies in this field is the work of neuroscientist Michael Merzenich, who is regarded as one of the pioneers in the field of neuroplasticity research. His work shows how repeated mental activities can lead to changes in the organization of the cerebral cortex. This is direct evidence that our thoughts and beliefs influence our physical state and brain functions, which subsequently affect our behavior and perception of reality. If we remain in a negative emotional state, whether consciously or unconsciously, the predominant state of our consciousness will be like a closed and continuous cycle. Our inner state simply influences over and over again what we see in the outer world. What we see in the outer world reinforces our inner state.

If evolutionarily older emotional states of guilt, fear, desires, or anger dominate within us, our thoughts will reflect this inner state and the outer world will be its reflection. A negative and even destructive emotional attractor automatically forms, which influences every aspect of our lives including physical health, work career, and interpersonal relationships.

Conversely, evolutionarily newest emotional states and high levels of consciousness influence every aspect of our lives in a positive way. These emotional states arose to support life, cooperation, energetic efficiency, and the survival of the greater whole. They have undeniable value not only for the individual but for all of society.

Conscious and unconscious thoughts that we repeat daily in our minds have a powerful influence on our inner world, thereby indirectly influencing what we see in the "outer" world. In other words, what we experience inwardly and what we observe around us originates from one energetic field. Our perspective on the world creates waves that visibly spread across the surface of the water. What happens in the outer world returns to us as echoes of the waves we ourselves have created. In this way, the inner and outer worlds constantly intertwine and influence each other in a reciprocal dynamic.

Everything is, however, connected more than the ordinary mind can imagine. It is not just about how our inner state acts upon the outer world. This aspect of human experience also influences how we are perceived in our surroundings. If our expressions are not in harmony with our inner beliefs, an inner conflict arises and our entire inner world is thrown into turmoil. The discrepancy between what we truly believe and what we express outwardly significantly affects our ability to achieve desired life goals and inner peace.
