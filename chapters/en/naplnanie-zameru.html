Your goals tell you what you want. Your intention shows you who you are.

Most people live in constant movement between what they don't have yet and what they no longer want. We chase after goals as if happiness were hidden in them, but when we finally reach them, we find that the sense of fulfillment lasted only a moment — or never came at all. And so we set a new goal, and the cycle continues. Think about it: How many times have we told ourselves that once we hit a specific goal, we'll finally be satisfied? And how many times has that satisfaction faded faster than we expected? If goals brought true happiness, we'd already be happy.

There's another way to approach life — one that isn't based on achieving goals but on fulfilling intention. Goals focus on specific results or states we want to reach in the future. They're usually measurable and time-bound, which makes it easy to tell whether we've hit them or not. Intention, however, is something much deeper. It's not a point in the future but a direction in the present. It doesn't determine where we end up — it shapes how we walk the path.

A goal might be getting a diploma, hitting a certain income level, traveling the world, or winning a sports tournament. Goals are primarily tied to external success and require long-term planning and discipline. When we fail to stick to the plan or our discipline slips, it leads to dissatisfaction, a sense of failure, and the guilt that comes with it. The disappointment is even greater when we don't reach the goal at all or veer off course completely. And paradoxically, even achieving a goal doesn't bring the lasting happiness and satisfaction we expected — it just pushes us to set another goal and repeat the whole process. This cycle is quiet, subtle, and that's exactly what makes it so dangerous. We feel like we're making progress, but really, we're just running in circles.

Intention, on the other hand, is about our inner attitude — how we approach our daily activities or life as a whole. It's a conscious decision to act in line with certain values. We can fulfill our intention right now, regardless of whether we achieve specific results or not. And the best part is that it brings us happiness, satisfaction, and positive results.

For example, when our intention is to become a better person, we can fulfill it every day. It doesn't live in some distant future. Whenever we help someone — listen to a friend going through a hard time, help a neighbor carry their groceries, give someone a smile or a few encouraging words — we fulfill our intention to become a better person.

If our intention is to learn a specific martial art, it works the same way. Whenever we show up to training, work on our technique, break down tactics on video, build our endurance, watch top athletes compete, or even rest and recover after a tough session, we're fulfilling our intention. But if our goal is to win a specific competition, it can lead to disappointment and frustration, because that kind of goal doesn't account for the complexity of life and the countless factors that affect the outcome. The goal lives in the future, outside of us, and is often dependent on things we can't control — the skill of our opponents, referee decisions, health issues, unexpected injuries, and countless other variables.

Unlike a goal, intention can be fulfilled constantly, which brings us happiness and satisfaction in every moment — not just at some point down the road. It allows us to enjoy the process itself rather than fixating only on the end result. And paradoxically, this approach often leads to positive results even sooner.

Take the story of Tainan Dalpra, a multiple-time world champion in Brazilian jiu-jitsu. During a conversation with the author in sunny California, he opened up about a personal shift that perfectly illustrates what living by intention looks like.

He shared that at the beginning of his athletic career, he was focused entirely on results. Everything revolved around winning. He admitted that he was often so consumed by the tournament and the victory itself that he completely lost touch with the present moment. Sometimes after a match, he couldn't even remember what had happened. And despite achieving everything he'd dreamed of — including the world championship title — he still didn't feel truly fulfilled. Even his greatest wins never brought the happiness he expected. Instead of satisfaction, all he got was a brief flash of pleasure that faded quickly and left emptiness behind. That emptiness had to be filled with yet another goal — just like millions of people around the world who unknowingly repeat the same pattern, hoping that this time the fulfillment will last.

The turning point came when he realized that victories alone would never bring lasting fulfillment. Instead of blindly chasing more titles, he started thinking more deeply about his intention. He asked himself, "Why am I even doing this? Is my goal just to win, or is there something bigger behind what I do?"

He began approaching training and competition with a new, conscious intention — to inspire other athletes, raise the level of jiu-jitsu as a whole, pass on what he knows to others, and be a positive role model not just on the mat but off it too. Every training session, every match, every interaction became an expression of that intention. Today, it's no longer about personal triumph — it's about serving something larger: his family, his team, his community, his country, and the sport itself.

You can feel this intention in his public statements. Tainan often talks about how much he loves training and the process of learning, saying again and again that winning is just a bonus. He speaks openly about wanting to be a positive role model for younger generations and help push jiu-jitsu forward — not just technically, but on a human level. What also stands out is how he talks about his opponents — with genuine respect. In elite sports, especially combat sports, that's far from a given. He doesn't see them as obstacles to be defeated at all costs, but as equal partners who push him to grow.

Winning stopped being a way to prove his worth. It became a natural byproduct of living true to deeper principles and a higher intention.

This inner shift also transformed his emotional life. That kind of change is typical of consciousness evolving toward Oneness. More evolved emotional states — gratitude, humility, empathy, compassion, joy, love, inner peace — begin to awaken in a person. It stops being about acquiring something and becomes about giving something.

Sometimes it's worth pausing for a moment and asking ourselves the same question: Why am I doing what I'm doing?

This principle doesn't just apply to elite athletes. It applies to everyone. A schoolteacher or a businessman can bring it into their lives just as well.

Think of a teacher who once measured her success by student grades, how much of the curriculum she covered, or feedback from her administrators. She can completely transform her approach. Once she realizes that her true intention isn't just to "teach the material" but to spark curiosity in children, a love of discovery, and confidence in their own abilities, her work becomes deeply fulfilling. From that moment on, every day is a chance to live out that higher intention. Fulfillment doesn't come after exams or at the end of the school year — it comes in every conversation, every smile, every word of encouragement, every moment when a small spark of understanding lights up in a student's eyes.

The same goes for a businessman who was used to tracking nothing but numbers, profit, and growth. When his intention becomes creating value, helping clients grow, or delivering solutions that improve people's lives, he starts approaching his work differently. Every meeting, every decision, every project becomes a chance to fulfill that intention. Fulfillment doesn't come only after hitting targets — it comes from the meaning of the work itself.

Paradoxically, it's precisely when we stop focusing exclusively on results that they start showing up on their own. Not as a goal that has to be achieved at any cost, but as a natural consequence of work guided by intention.

For people looking for a more grounded approach to life — one built on lasting values rather than a shallow focus on what they want to achieve — this shift in perspective can be a powerful liberation. It frees them from the endless cycle of desire and reward, followed by frustration and emptiness. People who live guided by their intention don't need to prove their worth to anyone. Their presence speaks for itself. And it's also true that small steps taken with clear intention carry more weight than big leaps taken without it.

This idea can also help explain why some people are constantly searching for something more and are never satisfied with where they are. They live under the illusion that happiness is hidden in what they don't yet have — which keeps real satisfaction permanently out of reach, always one step ahead. But it can also help us understand why some people who live in line with their intention achieve such remarkable results.

Some of them shape the world quietly. Others leave a mark the whole world can see.
