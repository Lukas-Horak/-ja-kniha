Your goals tell you what you want. Your intention shows you who you are.

Most people live in constant movement between what they don't have yet and what they no longer want. We chase after goals as if happiness were hidden in them, but when we finally achieve them, we discover that the sense of fulfillment lasted only a moment or never came at all. And so we set a new goal so that this endless cycle can continue. Let's think for a moment. How many times have we told ourselves that once we achieve a specific goal, we will finally be satisfied? And how many times has the feeling of satisfaction faded faster than we expected? If goals brought true happiness, we would already be happy.

There is an alternative approach to life that is not based on achieving goals but on fulfilling intention. Goals, you see, focus on specific results or specific states that we want to achieve in the future. They are usually measurable and time-bound, which allows us to determine precisely whether we have fulfilled them or not. Intention, however, is something much deeper. It is not a point in the future but a direction in the present. It doesn't determine where we should arrive but rather how we want to walk.

A goal might be, for example, obtaining a diploma, reaching a certain financial reward, traveling the world, or winning a sports tournament. Goals are primarily associated with external success and require long-term planning and discipline. When we fail to stick to our plan or weaken in our discipline, it leads to feelings of dissatisfaction, failure, and subsequent inner guilt. Even greater disappointment comes when we don't achieve the set goal or completely deviate from it. Paradoxically, even successfully achieving a goal does not bring the expected feelings of lasting happiness and satisfaction; instead, it forces us to set another goal and repeat everything. This cycle is quiet, inconspicuous, and therefore so dangerous. A person feels they are making progress, but in reality, they are running in circles.

Conversely, intention relates to our inner attitude and how we approach daily activities or life as a whole. It is a conscious decision to act in accordance with certain values. We can fulfill our intention immediately, regardless of whether we achieve specific results or not. And best of all, it brings us happiness, satisfaction, and results.

For example, when our intention is to become a better person, we can fulfill it every day. It is not planted in the distant future. Whenever we help someone—listen to a friend going through a difficult time, help a neighbor with heavy shopping, give someone a smile or a few encouraging words—we fulfill our intention to become a better person.

If our intention is to learn a specific martial art, we can likewise fulfill it every day. Whenever we attend training, work on perfecting techniques, study tactics through videos, build our endurance, watch top athletes fight, or even recover after a tough training session, we fulfill our intention. However, if our goal is to win a specific competition, it can lead to disappointment and feelings of dissatisfaction, because this goal does not take into account the complexity of life and the countless connections that affect the final result. The goal is placed in the future, exists outside of us, and is often dependent on many factors beyond our control—these might include the abilities of competitors, decisions of referees, health problems, unexpected injuries, and countless other factors.

Unlike a goal, intention can be fulfilled constantly, which brings us happiness and satisfaction in every moment, not in the future. It allows us to enjoy the process itself and not fixate only on the final result. Paradoxically, with this approach we can often achieve positive results even sooner.

An example can be the true story of triple world champion in Brazilian jiu jitsu, Tainan Dalpra. During a conversation with the author in sunny California, he openly shared his inner transformation, which precisely reflects the qualities characteristic of fulfilling intention.

In a friendly conversation, he frankly explained that in the beginning of his sports career, he focused exclusively on achieving results. Everything was subordinated to the goal of winning. He admitted that he was often so consumed by the tournament and victory itself that he completely lost contact with the present moment. Sometimes after a match he didn't even remember exactly what had happened. Despite achieving everything he dreamed of (including the title of world champion), he did not feel true fulfillment. Even his greatest successes brought him no expected happiness. Instead of a feeling of satisfaction, there came only a brief flash of pleasure, which quickly faded and left emptiness behind. This emptiness had to be filled again with a new goal. Exactly as millions of people around the world do, who unknowingly repeat the same pattern in the hope that this time fulfillment will be lasting.

The turning point came when he realized that victories themselves would never bring lasting fulfillment. Instead of blindly chasing more titles, he began to think more deeply about his intention. He asked himself: Why am I doing this anyway? Is my goal just to win or does my effort have a higher purpose?

He began to approach training and competitions with a new conscious intention—an effort to inspire other athletes, improve the quality of jiu jitsu as a whole, pass on his knowledge to others, and be a positive role model not only on the mat but also outside of it. Every training session, every match, and every interaction became an expression of this intention. Today it is no longer just about personal triumph but about service to a larger whole—family, team, community, country, and the sport itself.

You can feel this intention clearly in his public statements. Tainan often mentions that he loves training and the process of learning, emphasizing multiple times that winning is just a bonus. He openly speaks of his effort to be a positive role model for younger generations and help move jiu jitsu forward—not only technically but also on a human level. Also exceptional is his ability to speak about opponents with respect and regard, which in the environment of elite and especially combat sports is not at all a given. He doesn't see them as obstacles to be defeated at all costs but as equal partners who push him further.

Victories for him ceased to be a tool to confirm his own worth; they became a natural consequence of living in accordance with higher principles and a deeper intention.

The inner transformation also positively influenced his emotional world. Such a shift is typical for consciousness developing toward Oneness. Evolutionarily younger emotional states such as gratitude, humility, empathy, compassion, joy, love, or inner peace begin to awaken in the human. It is no longer just about acquiring something but about giving something.

Sometimes it's not a bad idea to pause for a moment and ask the same question: Why am I doing what I'm doing?

This principle applies not only to elite athletes but to every person. A teacher in a school or a businessman can equally apply it to their lives.

A teacher who once measured her success by student grades, curriculum completion, or feedback from school administration can completely change her approach. Once she realizes that her true intention is not just to "teach the material" but to awaken curiosity in children, joy in discovering, and confidence in their own abilities, her work can bring her immense fulfillment. From that moment, every day becomes an opportunity to fulfill a higher intention. Fulfillment does not come after exams or at the end of the school year but in every conversation, smile, encouragement, or moment when a small spark of understanding lights up in a student's eyes.

The same principle can be applied by a businessman who was accustomed to tracking only numbers, profit, and growth. When his intention becomes creating value, helping clients grow, or bringing solutions that improve people's lives, he begins to approach his work differently. Every meeting, decision, or project becomes an opportunity to fulfill this intention. Fulfillment comes not just after successfully achieving goals but from the meaning of the activity itself.

Paradoxically, precisely when we stop focusing exclusively on results, they begin to arrive naturally. Not as a goal that must be achieved at any cost, but as a natural consequence of work in accordance with intention.

For people seeking a more holistic approach to life and more lasting values, not focusing superficially only on what they want to achieve but also on how they feel during their journey and how they behave, this change in perspective can be an enormous liberation from the constant cycle of desire and reward, and the subsequent frustration and emptiness. People who live in accordance with their intention don't need to prove their worth to anyone. Their presence confirms it. It is also true that small steps taken with clear intention have greater value than large leaps without it.

This concept can better explain why some people are constantly seeking something more and are never satisfied with their current state. They live in the illusion that happiness is hidden in what they don't yet have, which makes true satisfaction unattainable for them and always one step ahead. It can, however, also help us understand why some people living in accordance with their intention achieve such amazing results.

Some of them influence the world quietly, others leave a mark visible to the whole world.
