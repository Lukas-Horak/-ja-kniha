Desire and resistance are two sides of the same coin. Both motivate us to certain behavior, just in opposite directions.

Our desires and resistances are driven by the same instincts we could observe in the most primitive living organisms. They can easily trap us in an endless cycle of suffering, which fundamentally affects our perception of reality. We constantly desire something we want, or we try to avoid at all costs something we don't want. Desire and resistance are two ends of the same emotional state, because both are deeply rooted in our perception of what we consider desirable or undesirable. Both emotions are even governed by the same psychological and neurobiological mechanisms, and thus motivate us to certain behavior—whether it is resistance in the form of: "Yuck, I don't want that," or, on the contrary, enthusiastically: "Wow, that's amazing, I want that."

From the perspective of emotional reaction, resistance usually serves as a protective mechanism that warns us of potential risk or possible harm, while desire draws us toward achieving something we consider valuable or pleasant. This dynamic relationship between desire and resistance determines how we make decisions and how we react to the world around us.

Of course, it has a direct impact on our daily decision-making, behavior, and influences us in all aspects of life. We often stand precisely between these two forces—what attracts us and what we unconsciously want to avoid. For example, we have a strong desire for success but also resistance to failure. We desire comfort and familiar surroundings but feel strong resistance to change. We desire to be perceived positively and have resistance to any negative evaluations or criticism. We desire to achieve mastery in a certain area, but small failures, which are an inseparable part of this process, cause us frustration, resistance, disappointment, and anger.

Overcoming this endless cycle of desires and resistances is key to achieving higher states of consciousness, where these pairs of emotions are transcended. A higher state of consciousness does not exclude emotional reactions but rather allows the individual to experience them without being completely controlled by them. Conversely, being governed by desires and resistances automatically generates stress, inner conflicts, and tension, as we constantly evaluate every experience according to our subjective criteria. We try to acquire and maintain what we subjectively perceive as positive, and we strive as much as possible to avoid what we subjectively perceive as negative.

Understanding the evolutionary significance of these emotions is relatively simple. The oldest emotions have their roots in the early stages of evolutionary development of living organisms, and resistance can be understood as one of the primary emotional reactions that helped organisms distinguish between beneficial and harmful stimuli.

For the simplest organisms, the emotion of resistance was essential at a basic level of survival. These organisms avoided harmful substances or environments through simple mechanisms such as chemotaxis or phototaxis, which allowed them to survive in hostile conditions. Resistance served as a simple way to avoid toxic substances or environments that could be harmful to the organism.

In more complex organisms, such as mammals, the emotion of resistance began to take on a more complex form, associated with avoiding harmful situations, aggressive individuals, or dangerous objects that could cause physical or emotional pain or even death.

For the modern human, however, in today's social environment, resistance becomes a double-edged sword. On one hand, it can still help a person avoid dangerous situations, toxic relationships, aggressive people, or harmful environments. On the other hand, it tends to prevent personal development, as resistance often stems from prejudices, biases, closed-minded positions, or fear of the unknown.

A closed mind, dogmatism, and low tolerance for the unknown are clear signs of the dominance of the "Primitive Self." Our "Primitive Self" always prefers old familiar patterns of behavior and thinking because evaluating new information and trying alternative solutions is energy-consuming. Conversely, higher levels of consciousness, where primitive and instinctive mechanisms cease to dominate, are characterized by openness to new opinions, holistic to transrational thinking, and acceptance of paradoxes.

The modern form of resistance, therefore, does not manifest itself only as a direct reaction to danger but also as various psychological reactions—resistance to change, new ideas, different cultures, or simply resistance to anything that disrupts our established patterns of behavior or our comfort zone. The emotion of resistance thus tends to control our decisions and influence our behavior at a deep unconscious level.

From a psychological perspective, resistance is often a reaction to a threat to our ego. Whenever something appears that could threaten our position or self-perception, whether it is information that contradicts our beliefs or direct criticism, our ego reacts with resistance. It is an instinctive reaction of our "Primitive Self," which can manifest as anger, rejection, or other unpleasant feelings.

This kind of resistance to change, criticism, other opinions, different people, or new information can take various forms from contempt through outbursts of anger to hateful acts. Daily, however, we can observe reactions in our surroundings like: I stand by my opinion and won't change it! Your opinion doesn't interest me., How can you think you know it better? I've worked in this field my whole life! Or: You have no right to criticize me when you yourself are not perfect.

Similar expressions are usually the result of deeply rooted resistance to any threat to the current state—our beliefs, opinions, position, methods, or personal preferences. They show how our "Primitive Self" limits our ability to open ourselves to new ideas, alternative solutions, constructive criticism, or another perspective. These basic instincts distort our perception, inappropriately trigger primitive "fight or flight" defense mechanisms, and automatically decide for us what is "appropriate" and "inappropriate." They also tend to automatically and without our permission create strong resistance to certain persons or groups of people, which leads to biased opinions that undermine our ability to see people and situations without filter and prejudice. In this context, resistance is not a passive emotional state but an active mechanism that shapes our perception of reality. In other words, our "Primitive Self" decides what is "right" and what is "wrong," what is "good" and what is "bad," what is "appropriate" and what is "inappropriate." Recognizing where our resistance comes from and consciously overcoming it is a key aspect on the path to higher states of consciousness.

In the second part of the book we will look more closely at how these opposing categories limit our ability to see the world without filter—as it truly is. To begin, it suffices to know that this dualistic perception of the world, based on desires and resistances, leads to a superficial evaluation of reality and a very limited view of the world. Overcoming the dualistic perspective opens the way to deeper understanding of ourselves and perception of unity and interconnectedness with everything that exists. Moreover, overcoming dualistic perception allows us to unite more with the world and with other living beings, which leads to transcendent emotional states of deep empathy, compassion, and inner peace. In other words, when we stop perceiving the world as "me against others," the sense of separation disappears. We realize that we are just different manifestations of the same essence. This realization naturally brings greater peace, more compassion and empathy, less resistance, more understanding, fewer conflicts, and thus greater inner peace.

Transcending the boundaries that our "Primitive Self" dictates to us requires enormous courage. It is a path full of challenges on which we must face the truth about ourselves, reexamine our deepest desires, and understand what actually drives our decisions and behavior.

Those who decide to take this step must exert unprecedented effort to overcome instinctive mechanisms that are deeply rooted in our brain. The entire process requires not only time and patience but also determination not to succumb to old habits and instinctive reactions that have long held us captive in cycles of behavioral patterns and temporary pleasures.

Overcoming deeply rooted mechanisms of the "Primitive Self" and the courage to face uncomfortable truths about ourselves leads to radical transformation. An individual striving to escape the endless cycle of desire and reward inevitably goes through a phase of resistance to their previous habits and behavioral patterns. On the path to a higher state of consciousness, it can be helpful to be aware of this phase of resistance.

To clarify, we can give a few examples.

A person dependent on social networks or the desire for admiration and recognition may, in the process of consciousness development, begin to feel resistance toward those who (unlike them) continue to expose themselves on social networks like monkeys seeking admiration and recognition from others. Once such an individual manages to break free from the pathological cycle where they had the need to constantly expose themselves for admiration and seek recognition in the form of hearts and comments, they may begin to look at similar behavior with contempt. This resistance, however, is not a manifestation of true inner liberation but rather a transitional stage. The person is not yet completely free, so they need to sharply distinguish themselves from their previous behavior and feel strong resistance toward it. One could say they are currently on the opposite side of the same coin.

People dependent on alcohol, food, sex, drugs, tobacco, gambling, medications, social networks, praise, attention, or control go through an intense cycle of desire and subsequent resistance during attempts to end their dependencies. Once they decide to break free from this cycle, the phase of resistance begins. It tends to evoke strong feelings of guilt, anger toward similar people (and toward themselves) and resistance to previous activity. Real progress occurs when an individual's perception becomes completely neutral, without any resistance or desire. If there is no negative or positive reaction in an individual, this emotional state has been transcended.

We can observe the same dynamic with other emotional states. An example might be fear. If the predominant emotional state of an individual is fear, on the path to overcoming it, they may unconsciously manifest resistance toward people who openly express the emotion of fear. Such a person is bothered by everyone who is afraid because they themselves have not yet come to terms with their fears. When an individual transcends this emotional state, resistance naturally fades. The low emotional state is transformed into a higher state of consciousness, where the individual no longer condemns anyone, criticizes, or evaluates, but rather feels understanding, compassion, love, or empathy.

The same applies to more complex emotional states, such as self-pity, pride, or other social emotions. Once we have transcended them, we feel no resistance because we understand very well how a person dominated by one of these low emotional states suffers. We feel only deep compassion, empathy, and understanding.

In the highest states of consciousness, an individual feels no desire or resistance to anything or anyone, which suggests not only deep inner peace and liberation from primitive emotions but also a complete transcendence of prejudices and biased positions. In the context of consciousness development, the ability to transcend these primary impulses and perceive the world without the need for constant evaluation and categorization represents enormous progress. The cycle of desires and resistances keeps people in a state of inner dissatisfaction, frustration, anger, and conflicts. From the perspective of consciousness development, this cycle is one of the main obstacles on the path to higher states. In many spiritual traditions, a state without the presence of desires and resistances is even described as a state of enlightenment. In this high state of consciousness, an individual is able to accept the complexity of life with all its light and dark sides. The liberation and inner transformation that result from this are essential for achieving the highest states of consciousness, where inner peace and deep understanding of life prevail.
