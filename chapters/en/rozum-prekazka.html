The intellect, which initially brings us many advantages, later becomes the main obstacle to our further development.

Our intellect is an incredible tool capable of amazing things. It allows us to formulate theories, create hypotheses, and subsequently conduct experiments to better understand the world around us. Through intellect, we discovered how to cure deadly diseases, travel into space, and observe the dance of subatomic particles. We have understood matter and its interactions. Since the beginning of the new millennium, the world has witnessed the discovery of smartphones, self-driving cars, and autonomous drones. Enormous progress has also occurred in energy storage, artificial intelligence, and medical science.

Thomas Edison, Galileo Galilei, Albert Einstein, Nicolaus Copernicus, Isaac Newton, Max Planck, Charles Darwin, Nikola Tesla, Marie Curie, Hedy Lamarr, and countless other significant figures contributed to humanity's scientific and technological progress through rational reasoning. Human history is full of inventors and thinkers whose extraordinary intellect helped deepen our understanding of the physical world.

Unfortunately, our intellect can also cause great harm. Through our remarkable intellect, we have constructed the guillotine, nuclear weapons, dangerous pesticides, we tinker with genetics, release viruses from laboratories, manufacture weapons and deadly toxic gases. Our pursuit of rationality sometimes leads to explosions of irrationality, and our obsession with thinking sometimes creates more problems than solutions. Human capacity to think, reason, and analyze everything has both advantages and disadvantages. The mind sometimes serves us; sometimes it does not.

As the brain evolved, newer structures (such as the neocortex or prefrontal cortex) were added to its primitive structures, supplementing our capabilities with higher cognitive functions—abstract thinking, higher emotions, the ability to plan, perceive time, or become self-aware. Recall that newer brain structures developed on the basis of old primitive structures, not separately.

High intellect, though often perceived as one of the most precious tools of human development, does not always function in our favor. It is relatively common for our intellect to serve as support and rationalization for primitive mechanisms. In other words, instead of helping us overcome immature, distorted, limited, and self-destructive behavioral patterns, it can strengthen them by providing justifications and arguments. Examples include situations where we use intellect to justify our egocentric behavior, self-destructive mechanisms, pathological patterns of thought, toxic relationships, or to justify fear, jealousy, self-pity, self-blame, shame, hopelessness, anxiety, anger, and other negative emotional states. Instead of reason serving as a tool for overcoming them, it can be used to defend or strengthen them.

Intellect alone does not guarantee integrity, emotional maturity, or psychological well-being. True wisdom and maturity come when we can use intellect to transform limiting patterns of thought and support higher emotional states that lead to genuine personal growth and authentic change.

The intellect, which initially brings us many advantages, later becomes the main obstacle to our further development. Some of us spend our entire lives perfecting our intellect only to discover it is our primary limitation.

It is common to believe in our rationality and logical decision-making. We humans think we can make correct decisions and our views are generally right, but from a psychological perspective, doubting our own views is unnatural. Our intellect tends to suppress doubt and ambiguity of information. Of course, deep admiration for human intellect, which has come a long way, has its justification, but we must acknowledge that it still has a few steps to perfection.

Very often we perceive as true even statements that are illogical. In everyday life, truth for us is anything that subjectively seems convincing. Sometimes we consider even the least probable and least logical things to be true. Our intellect often chooses the path of least resistance, and so we lean toward statements that support our thinking. It is not necessary at all for something to be practically provable. Our brain will find a way to connect information so that it strengthens our opinion preferences.

Logical and analytical thinking require mental effort. Mental effort burns energy and strains the organism. Recall that the human organism naturally seeks to conserve energy, and therefore often chooses the simplest path. Thinking that our decision-making is perfect and that we have our thinking under control is merely the view of a proud and conceited ego.

The Nobel Prize winner, cognitive scientist, and economist Daniel Kahneman thoroughly explained how our intellect sometimes completely disappears and is replaced by primitive and instinctive mechanisms. Paradoxically, one of the most significant thinkers of our time revealed that the human mind has its shortcomings while simultaneously describing how two distinct brain systems shape our judgments and decisions.

For people lacking information about the functioning of certain basic mechanisms of the human brain, it can be very surprising how often our judgments and decisions are fundamentally wrong. Daniel Kahneman's remarkable work revealed the extraordinary capabilities of rational mind (so-called slow thinking) but also the errors and distortions of primitive thinking (so-called fast thinking). The author describes the ubiquitous influence of intuitive impressions and primitive processes that significantly affect our thoughts and decisions. Thanks partly to him, we now understand in detail how our thinking is influenced by various cognitive distortions that create a filter through which we perceive reality. By recognizing the shortcomings of our own minds, we can protect ourselves from distortions and manipulation, which significantly reduces suffering.

Our logical reasoning, perception of spacetime, and ability to analytically evaluate information are provided by newer brain structures such as the prefrontal cortex and cerebral cortex. As mentioned, these newer parts of the brain are not separate from its primitive parts—they are built upon them. Therefore, our mental processes are closely linked to the animal world.

In the animal world, the foundation is the ability to survive, protect one's body, protect one's herd, obtain food (or its equivalent), monitor one's environment, reproduce, assess danger, and recognize enemies. These basic abilities were improved by evolution to increase chances of survival.

For an animal to have a greater chance of hunting its prey, it needed to begin perceiving where it was located in space. Of course, the ability to understand space developed over millions of years—various animal species evolved their sensory and cognitive abilities needed for environmental orientation. Over time, they learned to recognize where everyone was located. My food is "there" and I am "here." An animal orients itself in space in the present moment. It does not think about what happened yesterday or what it will do tomorrow. I hunt. I consume prey. I rest. I play. Some animals have neurons in their brain that activate when they anticipate a certain outcome, but their perception of time is much simpler compared to human perception.

In humans, the brain continued to evolve, allowing us to better remember potential threats, perceive spacetime more sophisticatedly (the prey is now "there" and soon will be "here"), predict seasonal changes (estimate the right time to gather food), analyze more complex information, coordinate social activities, or predict future events. Humans began to perceive time as a result of evolution and adaptation to the environment.

These "improvements" increased our chances of survival and allowed us to subdue much of the animal world, even though our body cannot compete with larger predators in nature. Moreover, our rational mind, unlike the primitive mind, can be governed by rules, hold more information in memory at once, or compare events according to various attributes. The rational mind can doubt because it can hold incompatible possibilities in memory (ambiguity). Conversely, our primitive mind does not tend to doubt, suppresses ambiguity, and can easily construct a complex story even based on minimal information, which leads to incorrect judgments and poor decisions.

New brain structures enabled us enormous progress. Through improved rational abilities, we could advance in the animal world and survive despite physical limitations. However, it is necessary to note that our intellect has its limits and we cannot always rely on it. Fast, simplified, and often primitive thinking has no chance of competing with slow, rational, and analytical thinking from the perspective of "thinking." Understanding these two different systems allows people to better understand their own minds, protect themselves from oversimplified judgments, pathological assumptions, incorrect decisions, or manipulation by individuals or media.

These two thinking systems, however, should not be compared at all. Each has a different task and they complement each other perfectly. Fast thinking has many shortcomings and limitations, but only in a certain context, under certain circumstances—namely in comparison with slow, logical, and analytical thinking. However, very little is said about the limits and shortcomings of our rational reasoning (slow thinking). Logically, how could we question our own mind? Most people cannot even observe and question their own primitive reactions, destructive behavior, or self-destructive mental patterns. How could they question their own thinking and logic?

We must realize that just as the primitive parts of our brain, its newer parts often do not function in our favor. Consider all our irrational phobias and psychological fears. Evolutionary improvements such as time perception and visualization ability automatically trigger stress hormone production based merely on our imagination. Our ideas about the future or memories of the past cause us anxiety, depressive states, and sometimes complete paralysis. Some people are virtually hysterical about a potential threat, even though the probability of it occurring is one in ten million.

Those same evolutionary improvements that brought people many advantages simultaneously cause them enormous suffering. It is very important to understand the disadvantages, self-destructive patterns of thinking, and limitations of our own mind so that we can move beyond the level of rational reasoning.

Although intellect is often celebrated as the pinnacle of human evolution and the source of our greatest successes, there exist spheres of existence where its light does not reach. We enter them through consciousness development that transcends logic and rational thinking. This is a very difficult step, achieved by only a small number of people. However, it leads to a deeper understanding of reality and the dominant experience of higher and transcendent emotional states, what is traditionally called a high state of consciousness or a state of enlightenment.

People who perceive the world from the perspective of the "Primitive Self" commonly identify with their body. This body is me. I can see myself in the mirror, after all. I even have a name. Who else would I be? However, if we shift in perception to higher levels, we begin to identify more with our mind.

It is clear and logical that if someone loses a limb, they are still the same person. A piece of consciousness, memories, or personality does not disappear with a piece of limb. They remain a whole being.

For someone who has transcended the "Primitive Self," identifying with the body will seem ridiculous. It is clear that I am a mind that perceives its body. I am not the body. How foolish! Mind, reason, and intellect then become far more important. The "Rational Self" has activated, a more advanced level of consciousness.

If we humans were honest with ourselves, we would acknowledge that we are even narcissistically in love with our intellect, our way of thinking, and our logic. We think we are the creators of our own thoughts, although in reality thoughts arise on their own, without our contribution. One moment we are thinking about "what we will eat for lunch" and seconds later we are nostalgically remembering "how someone addressed that insulting remark to us five years ago."

While thoughts are products of our mind, somewhere deeper in us exists something that is aware of these thoughts and can observe them from a distance. Who is the observer? This question leads us to contemplate that if thoughts themselves cannot notice their own existence, there must be some perceiving aspect of our self capable of observing thoughts and recognizing them.

Another shift in consciousness development brings a completely new perspective. I am not the mind. I am the observer who perceives my thoughts, emotions, attitudes, beliefs, opinions, and my body. We wonder, who exactly is observing all this? Who is the observer? The moment we realize this, new knowledge comes. I am consciousness itself. The "Higher Self" has activated.

We realize we are neither body nor mind. Identifying with our body, our mind, our emotions, opinions, or thought patterns seems extremely limiting and foreign. We realize that many of our opinions are not even ours. They come from childhood, when we blindly repeated everything our parents said or did. We realize that many of our thoughts and opinions are influenced by past experiences, films, our idols, media, conversations with friends, or ways of thinking we adopted from popular thinkers or temporary celebrities.

Through careful observation, we discover that "my opinion" is rather "one of many opinions" and we open to more objective perception of reality and evaluation of truth. We begin to observe ourselves and the people around us more, which helps us understand their behavior and thinking. The [Spectrum of Consciousness](#čo-je-spektrum-vedomia?) allows us to more easily recognize whether it is a defensive reaction ("Primitive Self"), a reaction coming from rational reasoning ("Rational Self"), or a conscious reaction with a dose of empathy, understanding, compassion, and love ("Higher Self").

We increasingly notice the products of our mind that are generated automatically, without our awareness. We begin to distinguish our negative thoughts, primitive emotional states, limiting beliefs, biased positions, destructive behavioral patterns, and incorrect judgments that prevent us from happiness, peace, and success. We begin to realize that even our rational reasoning comes from various programmed patterns that no longer serve us. They are no longer important to us, so we gradually let them all go.

We become observers. We have discovered our true self. A quiet and peaceful state of consciousness. The mind has stilled.

A quiet mind does not mean we have stopped thinking or miraculously suppressed thoughts. A quiet mind is fully functional. A quiet mind allows us to stop judging, commenting, and criticizing everything. In a quiet mind there are no unnecessary worries, prejudices, attitudes, primitive personal desires, or attempts to control everything. Attachment disappears. Selfish expectations vanish.

However, this shift beyond intellect is very difficult. Intelligent people do not want to abandon their way of thinking and logic. This is because the "Primitive Self" is still present within them (though in a more sophisticated form) and does not want something taken that it calls "mine." It would perceive this as a loss. These are my thoughts, my opinions, my values, my beliefs... Mine, mine, mine.

For the "Rational Self," these "products of mind" are extremely precious. A person is simply afraid of losing their identity. I am the author of these thoughts. They were created by my mind. How could I function without them? I would no longer be myself.

This is a mistake. Higher consciousness will not make us someone else. Quite the opposite, we become a truer version of ourselves. We are able to perceive our own thoughts, emotions, motivations, beliefs, opinions, and experiences more deeply. This process leads to increased self-awareness and helps us better identify unsuitable behavioral patterns and thought processes, and thus make intentional decisions about how we want to live our lives.

There is only one place where we find problems, negative thoughts, anxiety, panic, fear, jealousy, worries, limitations, regret, shame, guilt, or envy—in our own mind! It is difficult to accept, but our mind constantly distorts reality, often makes mistakes, very quickly switches to simplified and even instinctive thinking, and constantly creates opinions about everything.

My Intellect Judges

Our opinions tell us more about ourselves and our view of the world than about the things we judge.

We have opinions on people, their behavior, clothing, age, height, weight, IQ, gait, intonation, perfume, hair color, skin tone, number of rashes, marks, amount of hair, shape of fingers, length of limbs, or depth of wrinkles. We have opinions on their opinions. We have opinions on our own opinions. We have opinions on everything that exists. We judge, criticize, and form opinions on everything we observe.

Most opinions are formed from information we gathered from the tip of the iceberg. Below the surface lies an enormous number of connections that would influence our judgment far more than we can imagine. Ironically, we think we "know" something, but about the connections we usually have not the slightest idea, because we only ignorantly and egotistically hop across the surface. The higher the level of consciousness we have, the less we need to comment on the world around us.

The need to judge, criticize, and form opinions has deep roots in our evolutionary history. Like low emotional states, this mechanism was essential for our survival in primitive conditions. Our brain is used to rapid and superficial processing and evaluation of information. It is not capable of reassessing the entire context. No computer on the planet could do that either. We can only evaluate available information and based on it create stories about what is happening. The result is opinions that lead to many misunderstandings and prejudices.

At higher states of consciousness, we do not need to judge people or events in the world, comment on them, or discuss them. Discussions of this type usually only occur when we feel obligated to share our understanding with others or when we feel they need to be heard. Often it is a way of helping or guidance. The need or motivation to judge something, comment, criticize, or express ourselves in any way at higher states of consciousness does not exist. We accept reality as it is. We are well aware that reality is simply a reflection of the current state of things. There is nothing personal in it. An individual in a high state of consciousness does not approach reality with disagreement, resistance, regret, or rejection, which would lead to unnecessary dissatisfaction, sadness, anger, or other suffering. Instead, they perceive individual life situations in the manner: It is as it is, This moment is perfect exactly as it is, or: What is happening is meant to happen. These statements reflect a state of consciousness that is in deep connection with the present moment, without any need to bend, judge, change, analyze, or control reality.

A person rarely realizes that the information available to us is often isolated from context and connections. When we judge something or someone, we usually do so primarily based on what we "see." We do not consider connections at all because we do not know them and therefore (logically) cannot include them in our judgment.

Imagine we see a cloud in the sky. It is an object we observe. The cloud has a certain size, shape, external structure, color, and exists at a certain height. These are properties we can observe with our own eyes. Of course, the cloud also has a certain mass, internal structure, chemical composition, and other properties we do not see at all. These form connections that fundamentally affect what we observe. The connections include the entire sky, air temperature, location, distance from the earth's surface, atmospheric pressure, or the type of planet. Of course, connections are also formed by the rest of the universe and its development. The shape of the cloud we observe may change faster or slower. However, we can say with certainty that the formation of the cloud we observe at any given moment is enormously influenced by infinite connections.

The more precisely we wanted to describe the cloud, the more connections we would need to know. Our intellect, however, would not be able to process even the connections from the last five minutes. While trying, the cloud would change beyond recognition. Our description would be relatively superficial and would reflect only a temporary state in a very brief moment.

We cannot predict how the cloud will change. It is also very important to realize who observes this cloud. The observer is also part of the connections. Is it a child who sees the cloud as a dolphin jumping out of the water? Is it a meteorologist thinking about the amount of sea salt crystals in the cloud? Or is it an airplane pilot for whom a certain type of cloud is deadly danger? The description of the cloud would differ fundamentally depending on who observed it.

Our perception and judgment of anything and anyone is always influenced by numerous connections that are not visible or understandable at first glance. The reality we observe is only the tip of the iceberg and is always the result of continuous interaction of countless visible and invisible connections.

Similarly superficial and limited tends to be our judgment of people in our lives. Connections are key, but rarely available. Let us consider a chance meeting with a stranger who does not greet us upon arriving in the room.

What is key is what we see and what we deduce from it: That guy is an arrogant jerk who does not even know how to say hello. What do we actually know about him?

Like everyone we meet during the day, we see only a fraction of their world. Every moment in their life is the result of a complex network of connections. The connections include their family, friends, city, country, cultural customs, family traditions, life partner, weather, today's events, ethnic origin, breakfast, room temperature, health status, childhood traumas, personal desires, and the overall development of their personality from birth to today. An idiot, we think. We superficially judge only what we observe.

What if we learned that today they were fired from their job and do not know how they will pay their mortgage? What if we learned that their close friend died tragically yesterday? What if we discovered that they have not slept well for months because they work through the night to pay for their seriously ill daughter's operation? How would that affect our opinion?

To fairly judge someone, we need to know the connections. If we want to be more precise, we must go deeper. What do we know about their childhood? Do we know under what conditions their psyche developed? What do we know about where they grew up, what siblings and parents they had? Did they have parents at all? Did they experience any traumatic events in childhood? What is their IQ? Are they physically and mentally healthy?

In most cases, we absolutely do not know the connections. If we do not know them, how can we objectively judge someone? Let us think about this for a few seconds.

Connections include all the significant facts that allow us to more accurately evaluate what we observe. It is quadrillions of connections that we would need to include in our opinion if we wanted to be fair in our judgment. Moreover, judgment occurs from the perspective of an observer, but they are equally part of the connections. What do we know about the observer? What kind of day are they having? What mood are they in? Why do they have such an opinion? The state of the observer's mind fundamentally affects whether they will thoroughly understand the situation or, conversely, will be completely unable to understand it. Based on psychological knowledge, we know that people tend to perceive or filter experiences depending on their past experiences, fears, or injuries. Who then is the observer? Is it an unstable person who gets childishly offended regularly? Is it someone who gossips about everyone to increase their own self-worth or divert attention from their own shortcomings? Is it a balanced person who does not take others' behavior personally? Is it a fully conscious being who approaches people with compassion and understanding? Perception would differ fundamentally with each of these possibilities.

We will certainly agree that judgment will be at least highly subjective. In the event that the observer does not know the connections, judgment will not only be subjective but also superficially limited. We usually know the minimum of connections, if any at all. It is striking that our opinions tell us more about ourselves and our view of the world than about the things we judge. If we judge someone as a fool, it says nothing about them. Who this person is is not dependent on our judgment. Our judgment tells us nothing about them. However, it tells us a great deal about ourselves.

Why do we need to judge, criticize, and burden our mind with superficial opinions in the first place? What is the point? Do we need to have an opinion on everything? What if we focused more on deeper connections and less on what we superficially observe? Would it not help bring more understanding, empathy, and peace to our lives? What if we stopped having constant opinions on everything and everyone?

The world functioned long before we arrived with our important opinions, and it will function long after us. To stop judging, evaluating, commenting, or expressing ourselves about things is an enormous liberation. Once we stop evaluating the insignificant, we can focus on what is truly important. The moment we stop judging, evaluating, or criticizing everything, our mind becomes calm.

A calm mind is able to see what an ordinary mind cannot see. A calm mind is a highly efficient mind. A calm and quiet mind is able to perceive things that a mind full of opinions, judgments, comments, criticism, comparisons, evaluations, doubts, desires, and attachments cannot perceive.

Dualistic Perception

Black-and-white perception of the world limits our ability to understand the wide range of nuances that exist between two illusory opposites.

Ordinary human minds perceive the world in two opposing categories—good/evil, fast/slow, right/wrong, success/failure, young/old, rich/poor, beautiful/ugly, strong/weak, and so on. This dualistic way of thinking shapes many of our opinions, beliefs, and positions, which are not only completely unnecessary but fundamentally completely wrong. Dualistic thinking (sometimes also called binary thinking) leads people to simplify the actual complexity of the world around us. This black-and-white perception limits our ability to understand the wide range of nuances that exist between two illusory opposites existing only in our heads.

Dualistic thinking was necessary for survival in the wild because it helped us rapidly and efficiently process large amounts of information and simplify them into contrasting categories. This evolutionary mechanism helped us distinguish simplified opposites like friend/enemy, food/poison, safe/dangerous, leading to faster decision-making in emergency situations. Similar contrasting categories were useful in the animal world, but in today's world they prevent us from understanding broader context. In reality, these opposites do not exist; they are misleading and limiting. They are illusions created by our own minds. True reality exists beyond mind.

The realization that dualistic perception is simplified and very limited is an important step on the path to transcending our own mind. Precisely transcending mind allows us to experience reality as it appears, rather than filtering it through illusory and simplified black-and-white perception.

Reality is actually not black and white. It is a kaleidoscope of infinite colors, tones, and nuances. Dualistic perception binds us to divide various life experiences, events, encounters, emotions, thoughts, opinions, relationships, activities, objects, or people into absolute categories—good/evil, right/wrong, small/large, positive/negative. However, with this approach we extremely reduce actual rich reality. In fact, things are far more complex and often full of contradictions that cannot be simply categorized into two opposing categories. The shift from dualistic to holistic perception allows us to recognize and appreciate this complexity and diversity, thus opening us to deeper understanding of reality.

Thanks to neurological studies, we know that the human brain functions by efficiently processing information through black-and-white categorization. Research in neuroscience has also shown that our minds naturally distinguish between "us" and "them." This tendency toward social identification and group creation (in-group, out-group bias) often underlies disharmony, prejudices, discrimination, and various conflicts. It can also contribute to ethnocentrism, xenophobia, and racism. Furthermore, it affects our decision-making in social and political situations and contributes to conflicts at work, school, and other community settings.

If we are driving a car and see a pedestrian at a crossing, we do not have many options—we either stop or continue. We need to make a quick and concrete decision. To choose one of the opposing options. However, this dualistic way of thinking significantly limits our perception in complex aspects of life, especially in situations requiring empathy, deeper understanding of a problem, or finding holistic solutions.

Complex life problems transcend dualistic limitations and require deeper understanding, complex and multidimensional thinking that allows us to recognize nonlinear relationships and the interconnectedness of everything around us. Since our mind prefers simple opposites and constantly tries to conserve energy through mental shortcuts, it often prevents us from seeing the complexity and diversity of the world around us. In the context of interpersonal relationships, where empathy and understanding are essential, dualistic perception leads us to generalization and biased judgment of others.

Immediately categorizing a person into one of two opposing categories does not allow us to see what lies beneath the surface. By immediately dividing people into "good" and "bad," we lose the ability to see them as whole beings. This view prevents us from empathizing with others' perspectives, understanding their motivations and needs.

Perception of the world through which we see only ourselves and our needs is very similar to primitive animals trying to survive in the wild. Although dualistic perception may have been an adaptive advantage in the past in the context of survival, in the modern world it is important to overcome these evolutionarily older patterns of thinking and learn to perceive the world far more complexly.

Imagine we are evaluating our partner, family member, colleague at work, or neighbor. In certain life moments, we may categorically decide whether this person is "good" or "bad." They did something "right" or "wrong." Their behavior is "appropriate" or "inappropriate." If we approach this question dualistically, we can perceive people around us in a very black-and-white perspective—either they are "good" or "bad," "right" or "wrong," "appropriate" or "inappropriate." Such thinking can lead to generalizations and unfair judgments that categorize characteristics, overlook complicated circumstances, and ignore countless connections. We also do not consider where the person is in the process of their individual development.

If we manage to overcome dualistic thinking, we can see a specific person in a broader context. Suddenly we discover they not only have "good" and "bad" sides, but are a complex and dynamic set of characteristics on an infinite scale and are currently located "somewhere" on the timeline of their development.

A concrete example from everyday life could help us better understand the limitations of dualistic perception. Imagine we have a colleague who is very thorough in their work. If we approach their characteristics dualistically, we can see them exclusively as a "slow" and "inefficient" worker who is always holding everyone up. This one-sided perspective stemming from two poles prevents us from seeing their strengths and appreciating their ability to solve problems thoroughly. When we remove our dualistic glasses, our view of their abilities can fundamentally change. We realize that although they are slower, thoroughness is actually their strength and we should perceive it as part of one whole. Perhaps we are sometimes bothered by their "slower" pace, but at the same time we can appreciate that they are precise and notice details that no one else would notice.

From dualistic evaluation of "slow" becomes "slower," until finally we replace it with words like "thorough," "hardworking," or "attentive." Upon closer analysis (or deeper self-reflection), we discover that it is not even so much a matter of judging our colleague's characteristics but rather about our unrealistic expectations, distorted perceptions, or lack of patience. When we learn to see people and situations with greater breadth and depth, we can gain a more balanced, accurate, mature, and aware perspective on the world around us.

Realizing dualistic perception is often a key step in building deeper understanding toward the world around us, which would otherwise remain incomplete and superficial. This awareness gradually leads to minimizing negative emotions, experiencing higher emotional states more frequently, and overall improving the quality of our lives. We become more tolerant, empathetic, and peaceful.

After this phase comes an even deeper realization. We discover that dualistic perception does not need to be replaced with non-dualistic perception because both ways of perceiving are part of one and the same reality. They are two sides of one coin and one does not need to replace the other. Just as day and night, inhale and exhale, or light and darkness are only different manifestations of one continuous cycle. They are mutually dependent and create one coherent system. We know that day cannot exist without night and an inhale is necessary for an exhale.

By expanding our consciousness, we realize that everything is connected in one infinite and indivisible whole.
