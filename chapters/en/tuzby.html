The desire to have more, to be better, to be right, or to control everything stems from basic primitive survival mechanisms.

We constantly desire something—a better job, an ideal partner, an exotic vacation, a greater experience, longer rest—and when we finally have it, we desire something else. Desire motivates us to get out of bed in the morning, but in the evening it drives us to reach for our phones to see if someone else has something better than what we have. We always feel that the object of our desire is just around the corner. "Oh, if only I had more time, everything would be different.", "If I earned a little more, I could finally breathe.", "Once I find my true partner, I'll finally be happy.", "Just a few kilos down and I'll be happy with myself!" It is like chasing your shadow. We think that if we take a few more steps, we will finally catch it, but it always moves a bit further.

Desire is one of the oldest instincts that has evolved in humans into an extremely sophisticated mechanism. Desires manifest not only in the effort to meet tangible needs, such as food or shelter, but also in the need for recognition, power, social status, or intellectual growth. Of course, basic desires have their justification, but if they become the predominant emotional state of an individual, they fundamentally influence how they perceive the world, how they behave, and how they think.

Advanced human desires can thus be understood as a sophisticated extension of biological instincts, which, thanks to higher cognitive abilities, are even connected to the ability to think, plan, make decisions, or perceive ourselves.

Let's look briefly at how this emotion formed during evolution.

In the simplest organisms, desire along with aversion manifested through basic biological mechanisms, such as chemotaxis (movement toward nutrients and away from harmful substances) or phototaxis (movement toward light and away from light).

In more complex organisms, it was the effort to secure food, reproduction, and protection of territory, which represents the basic pillars of survival and reproduction.

These desires were and still are governed by the evolutionarily oldest signals that help organisms optimize their chances of survival and reproduction.

In modern humans today, a basic biological mechanism that was supposed to motivate an individual to meet basic needs evolved into an elaborate psychological mechanism of unfulfillable desires, mental and physical dependencies, unattainable expectations, and egocentric notions of how the world should function.

However, desires are not just an individual matter; they have become a tool that feeds entire industries. For example, the advertising industry targets our desire for recognition, beauty, power, or success and creates the impression that we can fulfill it by buying a certain product. Just choose the right perfume, car, or clothing. Ancient instinctive mechanisms are also exploited on a large scale by social networks, which deliberately work with the desire for recognition and attention. The entertainment industry offers us not only temporary escape from reality, but also exploits our desire for adventure and emotional excitement. It allows us to experience the full range of emotions with complete safety, in the comfort of our home or cinema. In politics, strategies are applied that target our desire for security. Politicians deliberately exploit them to gain support from people. The financial sector is built on the desire for wealth, fitness sells beauty and recognition, gambling offers the illusion of quick profit, and so on.

All these sectors function the same way. They arouse desire in us, offer quick satisfaction, and meanwhile gradually build unconscious habits and dependencies in us that keep us in the constant repetition of the same behavioral patterns and seeking the next instant pleasure.

The mechanism of desire originated to help us survive. At first it was mainly the desire to acquire energy from food. Over time, our desires evolved into a complex form, but the mechanism behind them remained the same.

In very simple terms, primitive parts of our brain force us to seek means that will ensure our survival. For the same reason, we feel the need to acquire and hoard possessions. Originally it was mainly the need to acquire food, water, shelter, or tools for survival. In the modern world, however, these basic needs have been transferred to material and symbolic resources. Our "Primitive Self" tells us: You really need a bigger house, a more luxurious car, and more pairs of sneakers. Property and money have become the equivalent of food and shelter. They create a false sense of security, stability, and increased capacity for survival. Our brain evaluates hoarding property, money, or even various social statuses, such as fame, success, or admiration, as securing our basic needs, which rewards us with a feeling of satisfaction and pleasure. Our brain can assemble the best arguments for why we need more expensive watches, a newer phone, a deeper pool, more fame, more attention, more admiration, more control, and so on.

For many people, property and social statuses have even become a kind of measure of their self-worth. The more they own, the more valuable they feel. This illusion, however, is merely a psychological substitute for the inner emptiness and insecurity that they try to fill with material symbols of success. Inner emptiness cannot be filled with something external, because its source is internal. It is like pouring water into a container with a hole in the bottom.

Breaking through the mechanism of desire with reason is very challenging. Rationalizing our desires, expectations, and modern needs can be extremely sophisticated, and without deep introspection, self-reflection, and humility, it is not possible to reveal this cyclical emotional state. Conversely, understanding these primitive impulses that influence our behavior at deep, often unconscious levels allows us not only to reveal this low state but also provides us with a more complex perspective on human existence and its development.

The cycle of desire and subsequent reward is extremely powerful, and therefore it is very important that we know how to distinguish well between what are personal desires governed by primitive processes and what are conscious decisions arising from a higher level of consciousness.

The instinctive cycle of desire and subsequent reward can grow into a cycle of suffering and unhappiness, in which a person constantly desires something. When they fulfill their desire, they feel relief for a moment, and this short-lived sense of satisfaction they mistakenly consider as true happiness. We could compare it to a person drowning and desperately trying to breathe. When someone finds themselves underwater and struggles for life, they experience great discomfort. The moment they manage to emerge above the water surface for a moment and breathe is an immediate relief from this discomfort. While such temporary liberation brings momentary relief, it does not mean it is true happiness. It is just a short-term interruption of an unpleasant state. True happiness is not achieved by breathing air for a moment. True happiness is found when we manage to get out of the water or when we learn to swim.

If we are looking for stable and long-term inner happiness, we must realize that it is necessary to make changes at deeper levels. It will not be brought to us by constantly chasing the short-lived sense of satisfaction that comes after achieving an external goal. This satisfaction is always only temporary and governed by biochemical processes in our brain. It quickly fades and must be replaced by another goal. Whatever the desire, at the most elementary level, our brain receives a reward after its fulfillment in the form of neurotransmitters such as dopamine, endorphins, oxytocin, or serotonin.

Neurotransmitters are chemical messengers and play a major role in shaping daily life. They transmit information between neurons. For example, dopamine creates a feeling of reward and pleasure after a desire is fulfilled. This neurological process always occurs in the same way, and frequent repetition leads to various dependencies—from food, sex, pornography, drugs, alcohol, video games, shopping, gambling, praise, attention, control, sleeping pills, painkillers, and others.

The satisfaction that fulfilling desires brings to a person is, however, only temporary. A person feels the desire to drink a glass of alcohol, and when they drink it, their brain rewards them with the familiar feeling of relief. They feel the desire for praise and after fulfilling it they experience a sense of satisfaction and pleasure. It is the same mechanism that has driven organisms in the need to acquire energy for millions of years. Evolutionarily, it is set to motivate us to behave in a way that supports survival. However, when the desire is fulfilled, the levels of chemicals in the brain return to their original state and the person begins to feel again the need to achieve or acquire something.

A large part of the population is not aware that this very cycle of desire and reward is the reason why people are imprisoned in countless bad habits. Many think they have their lives under control, but they are often governed by evolutionarily oldest instinctive reactions that hide behind the emotion of desire. I really want this. I really need this. I desire this. If this emotion becomes our predominant emotional state, it tends to influence many aspects of our lives. And if a person cannot observe this mechanism in himself, it will be difficult for him to bring it under control.

However, if we could look the truth in the eye with humility and self-reflection, we might be unpleasantly surprised to discover how much of our lives is governed by unconscious desires or habits ingrained over the years. By merely observing ourselves and becoming aware of the processes of the "Primitive Self," we can change much. The act of observation enables deeper understanding of primitive mechanisms and transforms their influence on us. If we don't try to hide this fact from ourselves and don't ignore the existence of the "Primitive Self," we have the opportunity to push aside many of our primitive instincts and significantly improve the quality of our lives. This is very important because all dependencies are based on the principle of desire and reward. At first glance, they may seem innocent, but they can easily deflect the trajectory of our lives and build habits that we won't be able to get rid of for the rest of our lives.

This applies equally to seemingly mundane things, such as mindless browsing of social networks, workaholism, regular overeating, or the insatiable need for constant recognition, as well as more obvious dependencies, such as alcohol, drugs, nicotine, or gambling. All these activities temporarily fill the inner emptiness, but from a broader perspective, they have a pathological character.

From a psychological perspective, it is often an escape from one's own interior. Constant stimulation of the dopamine system in the brain and external distractions deflect attention from having to deal with one's inner restlessness, existential emptiness, or questions about the meaning of life, which naturally arise in silence and solitude. People try to occupy their minds constantly with something so they don't have to face deeper layers of themselves. That's why whenever they feel emptiness, loneliness, or boredom, they escape into dopamine pleasure. And so they train their brain to seek it more and more often.

A person has a tendency to constantly desire dopamine because they want to repeatedly experience the feeling that comes with its release. We simply want to feel better. This is one of the reasons why people take drugs and drink alcohol. Alcohol and some drugs provide a quick way to induce pleasant feelings. Within a few minutes, the problems that individuals struggle with for a long time disappear, and pleasant feelings, joy, even euphoric states arrive. People suddenly feel freed from problems, their mood improves, they feel relief from stress, are more communicative, more confident, and more courageous. If it didn't work, no one would do it.

However, anyone who has tried drugs knows well that these artificially induced pleasant feelings eventually turn into very unpleasant feelings, anxiety, depression, even despair. This solution is only temporary and exacts a very high price. When the effect of the drug or alcohol wears off, real problems that were only temporarily suppressed surface. Often they are joined by numerous other physical and mental difficulties such as damaged health, problems in interpersonal relationships, loss of a partner, loss of employment, or even physical death.

With addiction to drugs, alcohol, or other addictive substances, a person has only two options. Either they end this destructive mechanism, or they die. There is no other way. Self-reflection, acknowledgment of the severity of one's problem, and humble acceptance of help lead to life. Pride, denial, obfuscation of the problem, and self-deception lead to death. Someone who thinks they have it "under control" often doesn't understand how the brain works or underestimates the impact of drugs and alcohol on brain biochemistry.

It is playing with fire. In most cases, it leads to long-term or even fatal consequences. Moreover, the psychological and emotional impact of addiction is enormous. People often experience the lowest emotional states of shame, guilt, and despair, which leads to further isolation, depression, and changes in the physiological functions of the brain.

Despite all this, addicted people are unable to stop using intoxicating substances. For someone who has never gotten into this cycle of addiction, it is often something incomprehensible and unimaginable. However, a dependent person is willing to sacrifice everything just to experience these artificially induced feelings again and again.

It should be emphasized that in a high state of consciousness, a person never again needs drugs or alcohol, because they find an infinite source of happiness, peace, and tranquility within themselves. This is far more powerful and lasting than any imitation of a high state of consciousness artificially induced by external substances.

Worth mentioning is also increased interest in psychedelics. Some mistakenly equate their use with the process of raising the level of consciousness. Many people look for quick ways that would push them toward high levels of consciousness or answering existential questions without effort. In reality, there is no such shortcut to consciousness development, and it certainly cannot replace the inner work that is necessary for real progress. Although these substances can open doors to unconventional states of consciousness, they themselves will not bring us closer to high states of consciousness. In fact, they can take us away from them, because they weaken our ability to distinguish truth from illusion. They can even lead to psychological dependency on the experiences these substances bring. And that can be even more dangerous from the perspective of consciousness development because people mask it as spiritual progress.

At first it may seem like a fascinating adventure to many, but over time the spiritual path can be reduced to the desire to "get there again." And so the original desire for truth quickly transforms into a dependency on "magical" experiences that provide people an escape from ordinary reality. Over time, this tends to lead to an inability to cope with everyday situations and thus deepen inner problems.

Although these experiences may seem spiritually significant and appear fascinating, very often they can lead a person away from the essence of consciousness development and create further illusions. And illusion cannot be used to transcend illusion.

Another way people use to escape from their own interior while constantly stimulating their dopamine centers is uncontrollable fulfillment of sexual desire. Originally it was an instinct whose sole purpose was to ensure the survival of the species. Today, however, this mechanism manifests itself in a completely different context. Many men and women cannot resist the power of these evolutionary programs, and so their lives revolve around the succession of partners, the pursuit of pleasure, and the effort to gain recognition through their romantic and sexual conquests, which serve as proof that they are "worth it." It is a form of unconscious behavior in which a man proves his own worth through sexual successes: Look at me, how desired I am, what an alpha male I am. In women, this mechanism often manifests as a desire to feel attractive, desired, and exceptional. Both models of behavior are merely more sophisticated manifestations of the same biological mechanisms that once governed animal behavior before and during mating.

The difference is only that the modern human has wrapped them in psychological, cultural, and social layers, thanks to which they are less recognizable—but not less primitive.

Uncontrollable fulfillment of desires that gradually grows into a cycle of addiction is not limited to sex, drugs, alcohol, nicotine, or the search for "magical" experiences. Many forms of addiction are much more inconspicuous and discussed significantly less. For example, millions of people worldwide suffer from addiction to sleeping pills, particularly medications from the benzodiazepine group. These medications are often prescribed to treat insomnia and other sleep disorders, but when taken long-term, they cause destructive addiction, memory problems, reduced concentration, accelerated heart rate, and numerous unwanted side effects. And while this is a problem affecting a huge number of people, it is discussed far less than other addictions.

One reason is that these drugs are legal and often prescribed by doctors, which creates a false sense of security.

Opioid painkillers, which are often prescribed to treat pain, are effective but also highly addictive, which leads to serious addiction problems. Excessive use of opioids can lead to respiratory system problems, a series of social and psychological difficulties, and in extreme cases, even death. One of the most striking and tragic examples so far has been fentanyl, which caused an epidemic of deaths in the United States. Many people turned to it after an injury or surgery when it was prescribed to them as an ordinary painkiller. Unknowingly, they found themselves in a vicious cycle from which it is almost impossible to escape. Probably none of them intended to become addicts.

The irony is that people tend to judge alcoholics or drug addicts for their failure or lack of self-control, but these "critics" themselves are often dependent, whether on sleeping pills, opioids, stimulants, antidepressants, or antipsychotics. They often don't realize that regular use of these medications is not just a harmless remedy or a temporary solution to the problem. In many cases, it is actual addiction that requires treatment and attention just like any other form of addiction.

This double standard in social perception reveals how easily some forms of addiction can be overlooked or trivialized, while others are strictly condemned. Addiction to food, medications, sex, pornography, video games, social networks, shopping, or gambling tends to bring the same problems as the more widespread, "more popular" addictions. The fact remains, however, that anyone suffering from any form of addictive behavior, regardless of how widespread it is, deserves understanding, support, help, and adequate treatment.

A special category is represented by lesser-known psychological dependencies—on praise, attention, or control. They are associated with behavioral patterns that sometimes can be identified more difficult than those already mentioned. Although they don't have as quick a destructive effect as the use of addictive substances, they can create equally serious patterns of behavior that prevent us from happiness, peace, and success. Their recognition and treatment require deep understanding of root causes and often also professional psychological or therapeutic intervention.

Knowing and understanding these behavioral patterns can be immensely valuable. When we begin to become aware of them, we cease to be their victims. Knowing why we behave and act in a certain way is the first step toward freeing ourselves from many inner bonds that we have unconsciously created. This phenomenon is often referred to as the "observer effect." The mere awareness of behavior changes how it manifests. In simple terms, when we observe something consciously, it ceases to have power over us.

One of these lesser-known recurring behavioral patterns is, for example, addiction to control, which manifests itself in an unhealthy need to have control over one's own life and surroundings. Like other addictions, this one can have varying degrees of manifestation. The stronger ones cause serious problems in physical health and interpersonal relationships.

For some people, the need for control is caused by fear of uncertainty, uncontrollable events, or concern about negative consequences, which is a natural evolutionary remnant. Control over the environment and events could have ensured an individual better protection against threats in harsh nature and thus increased their chances of survival. It is thus a manifestation of our "Primitive Self," which has its roots in the animal world.

People with this addiction often try to gain as much control as possible through precise planning, strict adherence to routines, and have an increased need to control people in their surroundings. They often have difficulty with trust and constantly try to influence the behavior and decisions of others. When they don't have control over life situations, other people, or their future, they may suffer from chronic anxiety and stress, which over time also affects their overall health.

For a person with this disorder, it is important to learn to accept the uncontrollable aspects of life. The ability to recognize when control is beneficial and when it is better to accept things as they are is associated with a more developed state of consciousness. The desire to control other people, life situations, or the future leads to enormous suffering because it is simply not fulfillable. If we catch ourselves constantly trying to control our surroundings, including other people, we can try to consciously reduce these manifestations. We need to relax for a moment and allow life to flow without our direction.

Imagine a situation where our son forgets his shirt for practice. One option is to get upset, start criticizing his inability, and rush home in a panic for this "necessary" piece of clothing. What if we let it be this time? Perhaps the situation will resolve itself naturally. He will simply wear the shirt he has on to practice, or borrow one from a friend. In both cases, he gets a valuable lesson that has its own value and significance. Perhaps we will find it was necessary for him to begin approaching similar situations more responsibly.

Efforts to have everything under control often exhaust us more than the situation itself. Constantly solving minor "crises" creates an illusion of order, but in reality, it only artificially increases stress and causes tension. Many people secretly enjoy this artificial stress, drama, and tension. They will claim it is not so, but the truth is that drama gives them a sense of being alive and significant. Silence and peace are unbearable for them because in them there is no one to save, nothing to prove or solve. And so they unconsciously often create stress just to have the feeling that they are "living." Paradoxically, the more we try to control everything, the less we can actually live.

Like most addictions, treatment of addiction to control is usually addressed through therapy or various stress management methods so that a person can lead a balanced and fulfilling life.

Yet these behavioral patterns don't end there. Life can be ruined by insatiable desire for attention as well. Every unfulfilled desire and every unmet expectation eventually leads to manifestations of anger (which we will explain in a separate chapter). For example, our partner does not, according to our subjective criteria, devote enough attention to us, and yet we desire it. If the desire is fulfilled, dopamine is released and we feel satisfaction. Conversely, if dopamine is not released, we suffer.

Sometimes the desire for attention is so strong that people try to obtain it by any possible means. Psychologists in this context speak of a specific disorder called histrionic personality disorder (HPD). Its name is derived from the Latin word histrio, which means actor. This disorder is characterized by conspicuous and often exaggerated emotional behavior that may resemble theatrical expressions. People with histrionic personality disorder have a tendency to seek attention and be the center of interest, using dramatic, theatrical, and manipulative behavior to achieve their goals, which could be compared to a theatrical performance. They have such an enormous desire for attention that they will do anything to get noticed—just like some small children who cry forcefully, throw themselves on the ground, or create nonsensical problems just to get their parents' full attention. The first signs of this disorder always appear during childhood or adolescence, and if not regulated, they continue into adulthood. This is why it is important for parents to notice them and guide their children in time. If such behavior goes unnoticed and uncorrected, it can later deepen and cause serious problems.

These individuals have trouble maintaining longer-term relationships; in communication, they emphasize the importance of their own person; they need to be constantly appreciated and admired; they are inappropriately sensitive; and their strong desire for adventure leads to the risk of succumbing to drugs or promiscuous sexual behavior. The trigger for histrionic behavior is considered to be fear of rejection or abandonment and excessive effort to prevent it. Unfortunately, this unfulfillable desire for attention can cause precisely the opposite and literally destroy a romantic relationship. It is not exceptional when an adult woman or man demands attention by constantly creating artificial conflicts, artificial arguments, and superficial problems.

In extreme cases, a person prefers activities associated with fulfilling this desire over everything else and loses the ability to perceive reality or correctly evaluate the consequences that such behavior brings—similar to how people addicted to drugs or alcohol prefer the use of intoxicating substances.

Noticing such behavior in a partner, family member, or friend can help us understand their seemingly illogical actions. When we realize that it is not intentional harm, but an effort to get attention at any cost, we can perceive their behavior with greater distance, compassion, and don't let ourselves be drawn into unnecessary conflicts. Many accusations or artificially created dramas thus cease to act as personal attacks and begin to make sense as a manifestation of an unmet need to be seen and accepted.

Psychological dependency on attention, praise, control, admiration, or social recognition is relatively common and can have the same devastating effect on an individual's life as other, media-popularized, and publicly discussed dependencies. For some reason, however, almost nothing is said about them. Perhaps because they are quieter, harder to recognize, and people usually consider them more as personality traits than toxic behavioral patterns. Unfortunately, pride prevents us from acknowledging our own shortcomings, limits, and constraints. Yet their recognition is a sign of development and healthy self-reflection, not a sign of weakness. Dependent individuals often cannot find enough humility and self-reflection within themselves to solve this problem on their own. They claim: This problem doesn't apply to me. That's nonsense. Or: Don't be silly. I have it under control. This self-deception often becomes the main obstacle in their personal transformation, leading to failure, physical problems, problems in interpersonal relationships, unhappiness, and unnecessary suffering.

Being human is difficult. We constantly desire something, want something, or need something. The list of "modern needs" is endless. Even though we have incomparably more than the generations before us, we still feel something is missing. The desire for possessions, money, status, fame, success, admiration, praise, control, or attention is simply miserably interpreted as a basic need that, after its provision, rewards the brain with a brief feeling of satisfaction and pleasure. However, this mechanism leads only to a chronic sense of dissatisfaction, because it stems from a feeling of lack.

No matter what we acquire, what goal we accomplish, or what sum we earn, fulfillment does not come. It is always only a temporary effect, which is quickly followed by a feeling of emptiness and the desire to start again. In this way, an infinite cycle is created, which renews with each new success or accomplished goal. We are blindly convinced that with the next success we will finally be happy. This time is different. Once I achieve this, I will truly be happy. But as soon as we achieve this "ultimate success," there comes again only the familiar emptiness, which must be replaced by a new goal.

In psychology, this phenomenon is associated with the concept of "hedonic adaptation." It is a phenomenon in which a person, after achieving a certain goal or acquiring a desired object, soon loses the sense of satisfaction and begins searching for a new and bigger goal, which they mistakenly interpret as a source of happiness. For example, when they achieve a desired salary increase, this improvement in living standards may bring very strong feelings of satisfaction at first. After a certain time, however, they adapt to the higher income and begin to feel the need for a further increase in income to maintain the same level of satisfaction. The same applies to a new car, a new house, a bigger client, a more exotic vacation, a more beautiful woman, more expensive watches, and so on. This process leads to an endless cycle of desire for something more, something better, more beautiful, or larger, which causes people to be unable to achieve lasting satisfaction or happiness.

For some people, this mechanism is so strong that the feeling of satisfaction disappears literally seconds after achieving the set goal. We are not talking about a positive stimulus for constant improvement and increasing the quality of life, but about a destructive way of thinking that resembles more of an addiction. Constantly chasing temporary satisfaction that quickly fades and leaves a sense of emptiness is not really about improving the quality of life. It is about chronic dissatisfaction and ingratitude for what we already have.

Associated with this is another psychological phenomenon that people imprisoned in the cycle of desires experience—perpetual dissatisfaction. A person constantly seeks new stimuli and goals without being able to fully appreciate or enjoy what they already have. True satisfaction and long-term happiness, however, do not come from that next thing we acquire, but from how we perceive and appreciate what we already have.
